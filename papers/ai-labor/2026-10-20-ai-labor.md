# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-10-20

## 1. AI-based matching improves refugee employment in a double-blind randomized trial [Hiring]

**Authors:** Kirk Bansak, Jens Hainmueller, Dominik Hangartner, Jeremy Ferwerda, Elisabeth Paulson, Angie Delevoye, Nicholas Adams-Cohen, Ashwin Ramaswami, Selina Kurer, Joelle Pianzola, Michael Hotard
**Date:** first posted 2026-09-28
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35448
**Summary:** Between 2020 and 2023, Switzerland's national migration agency ran a double-blind randomized trial on roughly 2,000 refugee cases, assigning cantons either via an algorithm trained on administrative data to optimize employment likelihood or via a draw mimicking the existing placement procedure, with officers and refugees blind to the arm. Algorithmically matched refugees were employed more months over the following three years, with the gap widening over time (+2.2 pp pooled, growing to +5.2 pp at 36 months, about 11% above the control mean), and effects were larger for cohorts placed after COVID-19 disrupted labor markets. The results offer rare experimental field evidence that algorithmic decision support can durably raise employment in a high-stakes public placement setting, at a cost comparable to hundreds of hours of language training.

## 2. When Better Opportunities Backfire: Service Worker Responses to Parallel AI Deployment in Customer Acquisition [Firm Adoption]

**Authors:** Xinyu Fu, Shulong Luo, Narayan Ramasubbu, Peng Shen, Xincheng Wang, Yucheng Wang
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-19
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25603
**Summary:** Using over half a million customer-outreach records from a Chinese online health-insurance brokerage, the authors study a rollout that routed low-intent leads to AI voice agents while reserving high-intent leads for human workers, applying a regression-discontinuity-in-time design to trace behavior across the workday. Routing raised the share of high-intent leads reaching humans early in the day by up to 23 percentage points, yet customer acquisition by those same workers fell by about 5 percentage points on the first calls, as workers persisted less and moved faster through improved leads, consistent with a higher reservation value for continuing any given call. A calibrated decomposition shows this behavioral offset outweighs the gain from better lead ordering, indicating that reshaping the distribution of opportunities facing workers can backfire even when the opportunities themselves improve.

## 3. Can Labor Markets Function in the Age of AI? The Evaluation Bottleneck in Hiring [Hiring]

**Authors:** Itai Ashlagi, Ramesh Johari, Jon Kleinberg, Anushka Murthy
**Date:** first posted 2026-09-24
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30058
**Summary:** The authors build a theoretical hiring model in which AI tools help applicants generate and tailor materials, making those materials less informative and pushing Bayesian-rational firms to rely more on coarse signals such as prior experience. In this setting, inexperienced-but-compatible applicants are hurt most, since they lack an observable track record to fall back on, and costly screening can collapse entirely or default to screening only experienced candidates. The model shows firms can respond by inserting a cheap intermediate assessment stage before full screening, which endogenously restores evaluation opportunities for high-fit inexperienced applicants, implying that AI's main hiring-market friction shifts from application effort to credible evaluation.

## 4. Complementarity, Augmentation, or Substitutivity? The Impact of Generative Artificial Intelligence on the U.S. Federal Workforce [Employment]

**Authors:** William G. Resh, Yi Ming, Xinyao Xia, Michael Overton, Gul Nisa Gurbuz, Brandon De Bruhl
**Date:** first posted 2025-03-12; revised 2026-10-08
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.09637
**Summary:** The authors apply a retrieval-augmented-generation system, grounding large-language-model judgments in curated evidence, to score several hundred U.S. federal occupational series on how generative AI complements, augments, or substitutes for each role's knowledge, skills, and abilities. Generative AI mostly complements and augments federal work, and even the occupations rated highest on substitutability show only partial automation of routine subtasks rather than full job elimination. The authors argue this evidence cuts against workforce-reduction plans premised on wholesale AI displacement and instead favors targeted reskilling and competency-based workforce planning.

## 5. How exposed is work to robotics? Occupational, industry, and geographic measures [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Sebastian Sartor, Robert Seamans, Neil Thompson
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-16
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24267
**Summary:** The authors build the first abilities-based exposure measures for robotics and physical AI, scoring a capability taxonomy of 16 robotic abilities against the 52 O*NET work abilities with a large language model, then aggregating to occupation-, industry-, and county-level exposure indices. Robotics exposure concentrates in physically intensive, lower-wage, lower-education occupations and industries such as construction, farming, trucking, and warehousing, the opposite pattern from generative-AI exposure measures. Because the index captures technical relatedness rather than cost or adoption readiness, the authors frame it as an upper bound on where physical automation could eventually affect work, useful for anticipating which worker groups face future reallocation pressure.

- **Displacement Is Endogenous: AI Workers in a Search-and-Matching Labor Market** (arXiv:2610.14850) — a purely synthetic agent-based simulation (seeded runs, no real-world data) of AI entering a labor market as workers; no empirical validation.
- **Is AI Widening the Wage Gap? A Hybrid Agentic Simulation for Labor Equity** (arXiv:2609.33367, revised 2026-10-06) — a Monte Carlo agent simulation calibrated to sociodemographic data, not an empirical or causal study of actual wage outcomes.
- **Right Order, Wrong Scale: Auditing LLM Judges for Occupational AI Measurement** (arXiv:2610.02492) — a methodological audit of LLM-judge calibration against worker ratings; no finding about worker or labor-market outcomes itself.
- **What Jobs Can AI Learn? Measuring Exposure by Reinforcement Learning** (arXiv:2605.02598, revised 2026-09-27) — a new occupational exposure index that diverges from existing AI-exposure measures but reports no correlation with wages, employment, or other labor-market outcomes.
