# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-10-15

## 1. Better Together? A Performance Theory of Human-Machine Skill Policies under Generalization Difficulty [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Riccardo Zanardelli
**Date:** Submitted 2026-10-12
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.16863
**Summary:** The paper develops a formal theory of what human-machine teams actually gain from pairing a worker and a model on the same task, separating a "systematic" component known from each skill's own track record from a residual that is only revealed by observing both together, and showing routing and synergy are both capped by what the paired evidence contains. Applied to a public study of 227 radiologists reading chest X-rays, the authors find the radiologists and the model are near parity, the available gain is mostly this residual (not just picking the better skill), assisted reading performed no better than unassisted on average, and the best deployable arrangement is recalibrating the model alone rather than any human-AI combination. The result is a cautionary benchmark for "AI-assisted worker productivity" claims: pairing a worker with a model does not automatically create value, and organizations need to measure the residual, not just each party's solo accuracy, before redesigning workflows around AI assistance.

## 2. Right Order, Wrong Scale: Auditing LLM Judges for Occupational AI Measurement [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Harry Lyu, Neil Thompson
**Date:** Submitted 2026-10-01
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02492
**Summary:** Using O*NET-BENCH, an audit built on an existing survey of 45,796 worker ratings, the authors test 33 LLM-judge configurations across six model families against 4,501 held-out ratings to see whether judges used to measure AI's occupational exposure actually agree with workers, not just rank responses in the same order. They find most judges rank responses sensibly (a bare TF-IDF baseline nearly matches the best judge) yet wildly disagree with each other and with workers on the acceptance rate itself (estimates range 3%-98% against a true 61.1%), and that calibration removes average bias but still explains at most 8.5% of individual-level variance. The finding is a direct warning for the growing literature building occupational AI-exposure indices from LLM judges: ranking agreement is not enough, and the resulting occupational aggregates should be validated against real worker acceptance rates before being used for policy targeting.

## 3. Is AI Widening the Wage Gap? A Hybrid Agentic Simulation for Labor Equity [Inequality]

**Authors:** Zhongbo Hu, Zonghan Wu, Georgina Curto, Aocheng Tang, Yilei Shao
**Date:** first posted 2026-09-27; revised 2026-10-06
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33367
**Summary:** The authors build a hybrid rule-based/LLM-agent simulation calibrated to Chinese sociodemographic data to trace how repeated AI shocks reshape the wage distribution over time, something static models struggle to capture. Across 30 Monte Carlo runs and multiple LLM backends, both the top-to-bottom decile wage ratio and the Gini coefficient rise persistently under AI shocks, and a counterfactual policy experiment shows education subsidies targeted at low-income workers raise successful skill-upgrading attempts enough to partially offset the widening gap. The paper offers policymakers a tool for stress-testing redistribution policy against AI-driven inequality, though its conclusions rest on a simulated rather than observed economy.

## 4. Who Governs Data in the AI Era? A Computational Analysis of the U.S. Privacy Workforce in Job Postings [Hiring]

**Authors:** Ramazan Yener, Muhammad Hassan, Masooda Bashir
**Date:** Submitted 2026-09-25
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32030
**Summary:** The authors text-mine and topic-model 1,143 U.S. privacy job postings from LinkedIn and Indeed to see how employers are actually defining a role that increasingly sits at the intersection of law, technology, and AI governance. AI-related language appears in more than half of postings and is spread across compliance, legal, governance, and security themes, and the resulting 18 latent topics point to a shift toward hybrid positions that blend legal, technical, and AI-governance competencies rather than standalone AI roles. The study is a concrete, data-grounded example of how firms are reorganizing skill demand around AI oversight responsibilities ahead of any formal regulatory mandate.
