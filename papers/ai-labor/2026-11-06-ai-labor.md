# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-11-06

## 1. Experimentally Validated Digital Twins: Evaluating Workforce Training Programs in the Age of Big Data [Employment]

**Authors:** Raj Chetty, Jamie Fogel, Lawrence F. Katz, Kadeem Noray, Sonya Porter, James Reisinger
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-02
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03938
**Summary:** Using anonymized U.S. tax records spanning 1999-2025, the authors build machine-learning counterfactual predictions ("digital twins") to estimate the earnings effects of workforce training programs instead of relying only on small-sample randomized trials. Validated against 37 actual RCTs, the ML-based estimates track the experimental benchmarks about as closely as the RCTs track each other, cutting error by 60% relative to older parametric methods. Extending the approach beyond the experimental sample, they show training-program earnings gains vary widely by location and time and are largest when a program places workers directly into target-sector jobs, pointing to a scalable way to evaluate labor-market programs more broadly.

## 2. How Is AI Affecting College Major and Course Choices? Evidence from Finland [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Ciprian Domnisoru, Tuomas Pekka Heikkinen
**Date:** first posted 2026-10 (IZA DP No. 19056)
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/en/publications/dp/19056
**Summary:** Using Finnish administrative records on university applications and course enrollment, the authors examine how exposure to generative AI, following ChatGPT's late-2022 launch, changed students' choice of major and electives. Applications to AI-exposed fields, especially computer science and other ICT programs, fell and stayed lower; among non-ICT students, enrollment in programming and algorithms electives dropped (mainly among those without a strong math background) even as enrollment in AI/ML-specific courses rose, more so among men. The results offer early administrative-data evidence that generative AI is already reshaping how students sort into fields of study and technical skills before they enter the labor market.

## 3. Occupational Uniqueness and Wage Growth [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** R. Vestrelli, A. Fronzetti Colladon
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-20
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28133
**Summary:** Using roughly 196,000 task statements from the Dictionary of Occupational Titles and O*NET covering 1977-2018, the authors build a measure of "occupational uniqueness": how distinctive an occupation's task bundle is relative to other occupations, separate from how many tasks it has. Occupations whose tasks become less distinctive see slower wage growth, and adding tasks already common elsewhere, or removing tasks specific to the occupation, is associated with lower wage growth, while the opposite pattern holds economy-wide. The findings suggest how task change is distributed across occupations matters for wages independent of how much task content changes, which bears on how automation and AI reshape occupational task bundles.
