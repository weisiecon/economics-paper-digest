# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-11-03

## 1. Learning about Markets from Models: Evidence from an AI Hiring Platform [Hiring]

**Authors:** Hossein Alidaee, Tarun Khanna, Rembrand Koning
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-31
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01226
**Summary:** Studying a platform that builds firm-specific AI retention-prediction models for large employers of low-skill workers, the authors score every job application against every firm's model, not just the one it applied to, revealing how an applicant's predicted fit shifts across employers. They show firms systematically disagree about which traits raise or lower retention even within the same low-skill labor market, so an application's predicted "hireability" can swing sharply depending on which employer's model evaluates it. Because only a platform that pools the full applicant pool and every firm's model can expose these gaps, the paper argues such platforms generate a distinct economic value — a model-comparison externality — that should inform firms' hiring-model investments and policymakers' choice of which labor-market frictions to target.

## 2. AI evaluators favor job applications written by certain AI models, independent of candidate quality [Hiring]

**Authors:** Nicolò Pagan, Michael Andreas Ebling, Stefania Ionescu, Anikó Hannák
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-30
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41866
**Summary:** The authors build an auditing framework pairing eleven LLM "writer" agents that draft cover letters with nine LLM-based evaluator agents across ten jobs and fifty candidates each, isolating how the choice of writing model affects hiring outcomes independent of candidate quality. They find a consistent pecking order among writer models — locally hosted models are penalized regardless of license — plus a self-preference bias where evaluators favor outputs resembling their own style, both traceable to shared stylistic cues the evaluators reward or punish. In the worst model pairing, roughly 1.8 of 25 equally qualified candidates are filtered out for reasons unrelated to their qualifications, a concrete demonstration of how AI-mediated hiring can introduce unintended, model-driven discrimination.

## 3. AutoAnalysis: An automated, self-updating meta-analysis of human-AI collaboration [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Michelle Vaccaro, Abdullah Almaatouq, Thomas W. Malone
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-30
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.42001
**Summary:** The authors build a pipeline that automatically collects and continuously updates a meta-analysis of studies comparing human-only, AI-only, and combined human-AI task performance, currently synthesizing 1,955 effect sizes drawn from 420 papers. Pooling across this literature, they find that human-AI teams reliably outperform humans working alone but, on average, still underperform the better of the two working separately — meaning most reported "synergy" reflects gains over an unaided human baseline rather than true complementarity. Because the tool keeps ingesting new studies automatically, it is intended to give researchers and employers a continuously updated, quantitative benchmark for when pairing workers with AI actually raises productivity versus when either should work alone.
