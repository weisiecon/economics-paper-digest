# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-10-23

Only one paper cleared the relevance bar today; the rest of the in-window, keyword-matched harvest either lacked any AI/automation mechanism or lacked a recognizable economist's research design, so this digest is shorter than usual rather than padded.

## 1. The Delegation Divide: An Open Index of AI-Agent Delegability across Occupations and Countries [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Cristian Espinal Maya, Santiago Jiménez Londoño
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-22
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30752
**Summary:** The authors build the Agent Delegability Index by having two language models rate all 18,796 O*NET task statements against a preregistered rubric of delegation levels and barriers, then map tasks to ISCO-08 occupations and weight them by ILOSTAT employment across 146 countries. They find delegable employment share rises sharply with national income (22.5% in low-income versus 48.7% in high-income countries), that full task delegation is rare and concentrated in short tasks (around 12 minutes), and that the index tracks both existing exposure measures and observed AI adoption closely, with no wider adoption gap in middle-income countries than predicted. The paper gives labor economists a globally comparable, occupation-level measure showing the "delegation divide" is driven by the physical-task composition of jobs that poorer countries employ, not by unequal AI adoption relative to exposure.

## Excluded, borderline

- **Decomposing Wage Stagnation: Employment Reallocation, Wage Structure, and Demographics** (arXiv:2607.01561, revised 2026-10-09) — a rigorous shift-share decomposition of Japanese wage stagnation 1980-2024; no AI or automation mechanism.
- **Debiased Inference for Bounding Wage Inequality with Many Controls** (arXiv:2609.37412) — a double/debiased-ML econometric method applied to the gender wage gap; a statistics contribution, not a study of AI's labor effects.
- **Maintaining Human Verification Capacity under Automation** (arXiv:2609.38459) — a theoretical framework linking automated checking to erosion of human verification skill, illustrated with placeholder/template values rather than an empirical calibration.
- **Labour reallocation and production-network recomposition in the green transition** (arXiv:2610.04018) — an agent-based input-output model of climate-policy labor reallocation; no AI or automation mechanism.
- **Asymmetric Information in Wage Contracts: Experimental Evidence and Welfare Implications** (arXiv:2610.06827) — a clean field experiment on fixed-wage versus piece-rate contracts; no AI or automation link.
- **The wealth-to-salary ratio: a simple indicator of inequality** (arXiv:2610.24192) — cross-country inequality indicator built from an agent-based capitalist-economy model; no AI or automation mechanism.
- **Work that Counts, and Work that Doesn't: Labor Supply in Experiments and Real Work** (arXiv:2605.29832, revised 2026-10-18) — a real-effort experiment on wage responsiveness among Prolific platform workers; no AI or automation mechanism.
- **AI adoption, productivity and employment: evidence from European firms** (CEPR DP21082 / EIB WP 2026/02 / BIS WP 1325; Aldasoro, Gambacorta, Pal, Revoltella, Weiss, Wolski) — directly on-topic firm-level causal evidence (AI adoption raises productivity ~4% with no adverse employment effect and higher wages at adopting firms), but the BIS working-paper version is dated 23 January 2026, well outside the 30-day window.
- **The Early Impacts of AI on Employment among Recent College Graduates** (Fairlie & Wu; IZA DP 18945 / NBER WP 35796) — causal event-study design directly on topic, but IZA still lists only "September 2026" with no exact day, so eligibility for this window cannot be confirmed without guessing.
