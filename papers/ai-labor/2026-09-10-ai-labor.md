# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-09-10

## 1. Chance, Persistent Advantage, and the Generative-AI Era in Open-Source Package Careers [Inequality]

**Authors:** Hazem Ibrahim, Yasir Zaki
**Date:** Submitted 2026-09-09
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09687
**Summary:** Ibrahim and Zaki reconstruct 102.2 million open-source coding contributions from 6.15 million GitHub contributors (2015–2026), measuring each contributor's career-best impact by how many downstream packages depend on their work, to test whether the "random-hit" career pattern documented in science and the arts also holds for coding careers and whether generative AI changed it. They find a career's biggest hit is close to a lottery over its works, with only a modest stable personal-skill component (about a fifth of persistence) and the rest driven by momentum, and this structure is statistically unchanged after ChatGPT's release once ordinary cohort-aging effects are subtracted out (an estimated shift of +0.03, indistinguishable from zero). The result cautions against reading recent generative-AI-era coding activity as evidence that AI has restructured who builds an enduring track record or how career success is distributed on open platforms.

## 2. Bias in the Tails: How Name-conditioned Evaluative Framing in Resume Summaries Destabilizes LLM-based Hiring [Hiring]

**Authors:** Huy Nghiem, Phuong-Anh Nguyen-Le, Sy-Tuyen Ho, Hal Daume
**Date:** Submitted 2026-04-21; revised 2026-09-07
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19984
**Summary:** The authors audit four LLMs' generation of candidate summaries for downstream resume screening, producing nearly one million summaries under systematic race- and gender-coded name perturbations across synthetic resumes and real job postings. They find that while the factual content models extract from a resume stays stable across names, the evaluative language attached to that content varies with inferred race and gender in a way concentrated at the extremes of the distribution rather than in average scores, especially for open-source models, so the bias is easy to miss in fairness audits that only check central tendencies. Their hiring simulation shows this "bias in the tails" produces both direct discriminatory harm and a subtler instability that can evade conventional audits, arguing that evaluating AI hiring pipelines requires examining distributional extremes, not just averages.
