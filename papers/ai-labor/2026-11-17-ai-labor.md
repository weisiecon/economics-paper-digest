# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-11-17

## 1. AI and the Two Margins of Work: Why Job Losses Show Up Before Job Creation [Employment]

**Authors:** Mark Esposito, Liu Zhang
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-12
**Source:** arXiv:2611.17530
**Summary:** The authors argue that technological change hits labor demand on two separate margins — adjustment within existing activities and the creation of new ones, usually by new firms — and that payroll statistics record the first margin promptly but the second only with a long lag, drawing on patterns from computing-era entrant cohorts since 1978. They build a model in which an AI capability lowers the cost of existing output while also making new projects worth attempting, and show that whether hours fall in the short run depends on how quickly firms can re-scale production; using establishment job flows through early 2026, they find no clear gradient on language-model exposure on either margin, which the model says is consistent with the current, gradual pace of adoption rather than evidence of no effect. They propose a "net reinstatement ratio" and conclude that because new work created by AI shows up in the statistics later than work it displaces, standard payroll data will systematically overstate AI-driven displacement for several years.

## 2. Exposure Is Not Adoption: Measuring Generative AI and Labor Demand [Employment]

**Authors:** Ashish K. Sedai
**Date:** first posted 2026-11 (exact day not stated in source)
**Source:** CAMA Working Papers (Centre for Applied Macroeconomic Analysis, ANU); RePEc:een:camaaa:2026-97
**Summary:** The author argues that standard occupational "exposure" indices capture what generative AI could technically do rather than what workers actually use it for, and builds an alternative adoption measure from real usage data (the Anthropic Economic Index, cross-checked against OpenAI usage data) across 47 occupational sectors. Exposure and adoption rank occupations similarly on average, but adoption is far more concentrated in a smaller set of occupations. Using weekly Indeed job postings after ChatGPT's release, labor demand tracks this usage-based adoption measure rather than exposure: a one-standard-deviation rise in adoption is associated with roughly a 10 log-point decline in postings, while exposure alone shows no detectable relationship — implying that exposure-based forecasts of AI's labor-market impact may be identifying the wrong firms and occupations.

## 3. The Human Side of ML Engineering: Analyzing the Collaboration and Communication Skills in ML Job Postings [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Aidin Azamnouri, Phuong Thao Le, Justus Bogner, Stefan Wagner
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-13
**Source:** arXiv:2611.18915
**Summary:** The authors collect 1,000 job postings (500 in machine learning, 500 in software engineering) from five continents and use a human-plus-LLM annotation pipeline, built on a taxonomy drawn from prior work, to code how collaboration and communication requirements are expressed — including implicit mentions that keyword searches miss. They find collaboration language in 89% of ML postings and communication language in 72%, both well above earlier keyword-based estimates, with roughly a fifth to a quarter of these requirements expressed only implicitly. ML and software-engineering postings demand interpersonal skills at statistically similar overall rates, but ML postings frame collaboration as cross-role integration and communication as audience-facing presentation, rather than the team-practice framing typical of software-engineering roles — evidence that employers' skill demands for AI-related jobs differ qualitatively, not just quantitatively, from adjacent technical roles.

## Excluded, borderline

- **Can AI Judge You? An Empirical Audit of Deployment Readiness in LLM-assisted Hiring Systems** (Roy, Majumdar & Deb, arXiv 2611.20140) — a controlled audit of an LLM resume-screening architecture's operational and compliance behavior, but it measures the system's own consistency and manipulability rather than any actual hiring, wage, or employment outcome for workers.
- **AI-Mediated Employee Voice and Performance** (Tamme & Levin, arXiv 2610.25548) — explicitly a "research proposal" for a planned randomized field intervention at a consulting firm; no data has been collected or results reported yet.
- **Game theory of academic effort and hiring in the age of AI** (Pal & Park, arXiv 2610.35802) — a purely theoretical game-theoretic model of academic hiring under AI-assisted publishing, with no empirical test against labor-market data.
- **Artificial Intelligence and Job Design: A Dynamic Framework** (Shirangi & Pittinsky, arXiv 2610.28830) — a conceptual/theoretical framework paper drawing on organizational theory, with no empirical data or findings.
- **A Taxonomy-Driven Synthetic Dataset for Retrieval Effectiveness and Fairness Evaluation in Hiring** (Jouanneau et al., arXiv 2611.09178) — a synthetic benchmark/dataset paper for auditing HR-embedding models, not a finding about actual worker or labor-market outcomes.
- **Conditional Endorsement: Simulating How Adults with Different Skills Evaluate Canada's National AI Strategy** (Das, Leduc & Guha, arXiv 2611.19111) — evaluates AI policy perceptions using 3,200 LLM-simulated personas built from survey data, not actual worker responses or outcomes.
- **Beyond Exposure: AI and Labor Market Reorganization in Türkiye** (Nxumalo & Raju, IZA DP 19026) — a strong, directly on-topic finding using 2024 Turkish labor force survey microdata, but the source still gives only "October 2026" with no day, so it cannot be confirmed to fall inside the 30-day window (same exclusion as in the 2026-11-13 and 2026-11-15 digests).
