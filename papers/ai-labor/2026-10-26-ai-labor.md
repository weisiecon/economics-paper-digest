# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-10-26

Only two papers cleared the relevance bar today; the rest of the in-window, keyword-matched harvest was either a conceptual/theoretical framework with no empirical estimates, a qualitative or proposal-stage study with no results yet, or an otherwise solid labor-economics paper lacking any AI/automation mechanism, so this digest is shorter than usual rather than padded.

## 1. The Effects of Generative AI on Collaboration and Social Networks Among Knowledge Workers [Firm Adoption]

**Authors:** Giuseppe Soda, Alessandro Iorio, Leonardo Rizzo
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-23
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32461
**Summary:** Using 13 months of passively collected behavioral data on more than 12,000 knowledge workers during a staged rollout of Microsoft Copilot, the authors combine a staggered difference-in-differences design with a fixed-effects instrumental-variable approach to isolate generative AI's causal effect on collaboration. They find that adopting the AI assistant increased employees' overall collaboration time and broadened their networks to more colleagues, even as they also concentrated more of that broadened activity on already-trusted ties. The results push back against fears that generative AI substitutes for human-to-human interaction at work, suggesting instead that AI output's need for interpretation and verification reshapes rather than shrinks collaborative networks.

## 2. Working with AI: Measuring the Applicability of Generative AI to Occupations [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Kiran Tomlinson, Sonia Jaffe, Will Wang, Scott Counts, Siddharth Suri
**Date:** first posted 2025-07-10; revised 2026-10-23
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.07935
**Summary:** Drawing on 200,000 anonymized Microsoft Bing Copilot conversations, the authors use an LLM-based classification pipeline to map each exchange onto the O*NET taxonomy of work activities, producing an occupation-level measure of how applicable generative AI is to real work tasks. They find AI assistance concentrates in information-creation and communication activities, that applicability cuts broadly across sectors rather than clustering narrowly, and that occupation-level applicability rose over their nine-month observation window as people used AI for an increasingly varied range of tasks. This revision extends an exposure measure built from actual usage data rather than expert judgment or capability benchmarks, giving labor economists an empirically grounded alternative to task-exposure scores.

## Excluded, borderline

- **Artificial Intelligence and Job Design: A Dynamic Framework** (arXiv:2610.28830) — a conceptual AI-Job Dynamics Framework drawn from sociotechnical systems and organizational-learning theory; no empirical data or causal design.
- **AI-Mediated Employee Voice and Performance: Disentangling Informational and Affective Pathways** (arXiv:2610.25548) — a well-designed randomized field intervention at a consulting firm, but it is a research proposal with no results reported yet.
- **AI as Coordination-Compressing Capital: Task Reallocation, Organizational Redesign, and the Regime Fork** (arXiv:2602.16078, revised 2026-10-01) — a theoretical model of AI-driven managerial-wage dispersion with "numerical illustrations, not estimates"; no empirical labor-market findings.
- **Occupational Uniqueness and Wage Growth** (arXiv:2610.28133) — a rigorous O*NET/DOT panel study (1977-2018) linking task-structure change to wage growth; no AI or automation mechanism.
- **Does the Gender Wage Gap Originate at Labor Market Entry? Evidence from South Korea** (arXiv:2608.03153, revised 2026-10-12) — a careful selection-corrected quasi-experimental design on the Korean gender wage gap; no AI or automation mechanism.
- **The Early Impacts of AI on Employment among Recent College Graduates** (Fairlie & Wu; IZA DP 18945 / NBER WP 35796) — directly on-topic causal evidence (no relative rise in new-graduate unemployment, with some positive interaction between remote-work availability and AI exposure), but IZA still lists only "September 2026" with no exact day and the NBER working-paper number could not be independently confirmed, so eligibility for this window remains unconfirmed.
