# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-11-26

## 1. AI-Assisted Programming and Labor Demand [Hiring]

**Authors:** Lindsey Raymond, Eleanor Wiske Dillon
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-23
**Source:** arXiv:2611.31083
**Summary:** The authors study firm-level adoption of GitHub Copilot across 5,414 multinational firms accounting for 38% of the U.S. programming workforce, using firms' predetermined software procurement cycles as a natural experiment to identify causal effects. They find that Copilot adoption reduces quarterly technical hiring by 29% among review-induced adopters after one year, with no effect on separations, overall headcount, or vacancy duration, and the hiring slowdown is concentrated in junior technical roles with the highest share of routine coding work. The results show AI coding assistance causally shifting firm labor demand toward more experienced workers and roles blending coding with broader design responsibilities, rather than simply shrinking the technical workforce overall.

## 2. Will AI Replace Juniors? How Task Complexity Reduces Automation Risk [Hiring]

**Authors:** Vincent Rost, Fabian Stephany
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-25
**Source:** arXiv:2611.34099
**Summary:** Using a pre-registered survey experiment with managers from 1,435 German firms in the information economy and manufacturing, the authors randomly assign managers to consider hiring needs involving either standardized or complex tasks requiring professional judgment. They find that framing a need around complex tasks reduces managers' willingness to substitute AI for an entry-level hire by 0.54 points on a five-point scale and raises the stated importance of human oversight, while pre-treatment responses show managers expect AI to curb hiring of both entry-level and experienced workers, but far more strongly for entry-level workers. The design isolates task complexity itself as a causal lever shaping firms' AI-versus-junior-hire tradeoff, rather than relying on observed adoption outcomes alone.

## 3. AI, Automation, and Fiscal Pressure [Policy]

**Authors:** Spencer Bastani, Georg Graetz, Bernat Pla, Oskar N. Skans
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-25
**Source:** arXiv:2611.34234
**Summary:** The authors embed task-level automation in a two-sector general equilibrium model calibrated to EU countries, where labor is taxed more heavily than capital and public services are funded from general tax revenue. They show that automation shifts income toward lightly taxed capital, so public budgets deteriorate at unchanged tax rates even when automation proceeds equally fast in the public and private sectors; in their EU-27 benchmark, where market-sector automation outpaces the public sector, the deficit rises by nearly one percentage point of value added as the economy grows twelve percent from automation. The paper gives a quantified welfare/fiscal channel through which AI-driven automation strains government budgets independent of its effect on aggregate output.

## 4. How AI Impacts Skill Formation [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Judy Hanwen Shen, Alex Tamkin
**Date:** first posted 2026-01-28; revised 2026-11-23
**Source:** arXiv:2601.20245
**Summary:** The authors run randomized experiments in which developers learn a new asynchronous programming library with or without AI assistance, measuring both task performance and subsequent skill acquisition. They find AI use impairs conceptual understanding, code reading, and debugging ability without delivering significant average efficiency gains, and that participants who fully delegated tasks to AI gained some productivity but at the cost of learning the material, though three of six identified AI-interaction patterns preserve learning even with AI assistance. The results point to a causal tradeoff between AI-assisted productivity and human capital formation for novice workers, relevant to how firms should structure AI-augmented training and onboarding.
