# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-10-21

## 1. How exposed is work to robotics? Occupational, industry, and geographic measures [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Sebastian Sartor, Robert Seamans, Neil Thompson
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-16
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24267
**Summary:** The authors build the first abilities-based exposure measures for robotics and physical AI, using a 16-capability taxonomy scored against O*NET work abilities with a large language model, and aggregate it to occupation, industry, and county-level indices. They find robotics exposure concentrates in physically intensive, lower-wage, lower-education occupations and industries such as construction, farming, trucking, and warehousing — the reverse of the pattern seen for generative-AI exposure measures. The measures give labor economists a new tool for anticipating where physical automation could reshape work, while the authors caution they capture technical relatedness rather than actual adoption, cost, or readiness.

## 2. When Better Opportunities Backfire: Service Worker Responses to Parallel AI Deployment in Customer Acquisition [Firm Adoption]

**Authors:** Xinyu Fu, Shulong Luo, Narayan Ramasubbu, Peng Shen, Xincheng Wang, Yucheng Wang
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-19
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25603
**Summary:** Using over half a million outreach records around an AI voice-agent rollout at a Chinese online health-insurance brokerage, the authors apply a regression-discontinuity-in-time design to see how routing low-intent leads to AI and high-intent leads to humans changed worker behavior across the workday. They find the AI deployment enriched the leads human workers handled early in the day, but workers responded by persisting less with high-value leads and shortening calls, so the predicted gain from better lead sorting was outweighed by this behavioral response and customer acquisition fell. The paper shows that how AI redistributes opportunities within a shift, not just how much work it automates, can backfire on worker effort and firm outcomes.

## 3. Who Gains When the Umpire Is a Machine? Evidence from the Automated Ball-Strike System (ABS) [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Seung Jin Cho, Jun Yeong Lee
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-20
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27576
**Summary:** Using more than a million pitches from Korea's top baseball league, which became the first top-tier league to replace human home-plate umpires with an automated ball-strike system in 2024, the authors separate the effect of removing human discretion from the effect of imposing a new rule-based strike zone. They find the automated system eliminated count-dependent favoritism and the small rents catchers and star batters previously earned through skill and reputation, while the new height-scaled zone redistributed calls toward taller batters, with pitchers quickly adjusting pitch locations and batters showing no adjustment. The study offers a clean natural experiment on how replacing human judgment with an algorithmic rule reallocates returns to skill in a real labor market.

## Excluded, borderline

- **Occupational Uniqueness and Wage Growth** (arXiv:2610.28133) — a general task-structure/wage-growth framework using historical O*NET data from 1977-2018; no AI or automation mechanism studied.
- **AI-Mediated Employee Voice and Performance: Disentangling Informational and Affective Pathways** (arXiv:2610.25548) — a research proposal describing a planned randomized field intervention; no empirical results yet.
- **The GenAI Who Knew Too Little: Revisiting Transactive Memory Systems in Human GenAI Collaboration** (arXiv:2610.28343) — a qualitative interview study with 14 knowledge workers; no quantified worker or labor-market outcome.
- **Does the Gender Wage Gap Originate at Labor Market Entry? Evidence from South Korea** (arXiv:2608.03153, revised 2026-10-12) — a rigorous wage-gap decomposition, but with no AI or automation link.
- **Skill, Luck, or Imitation? What Actually Pays in Creator Markets** (arXiv:2609.32925) — platform-creator earnings dynamics on YouTube; no AI or automation mechanism studied.
- **What if automating AI R&D triggers an intelligence explosion?** (arXiv:2609.36054) — an AI-governance/policy essay on existential risk from automated AI R&D, not an empirical labor-market study.
