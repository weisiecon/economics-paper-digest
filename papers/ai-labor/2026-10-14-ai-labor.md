# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-10-14

## 1. When Less Is More: Managing AI Adoption with Adaptive Incentive Design [Firm Adoption]

**Authors:** Jie Gong, Jiayi Hou, Jin Li, Fei Pu, Xinjue Yao
**Date:** Submitted 2026-09-26
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32859
**Summary:** A large medical-device company mandated roughly 5,000 employees to submit at least 200 queries per month to an internal AI assistant, and the authors exploit a staggered rollout of a revised, lower target (100 queries) across branches to identify its effects. The original quota drove gaming rather than genuine use, with query counts bunching at the threshold and 31% of queries repeated or off-task, while the revised target cut query volume by 30% almost entirely by eliminating that gamed activity, leaving genuine work-related usage essentially unchanged and raising sales employees' monthly sales by 7%. The study shows that how firms calibrate AI usage mandates, not just whether they adopt AI, is a consequential and measurable lever for employee performance.

## 2. Understanding Interfirm AI Talent Flow Networks through Online Professional Profiles [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Donghang Li, Yunhan Zheng, Alok Prakash, Shenhao Wang, Jinhua Zhao
**Date:** Submitted 2026-10-06
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08264
**Summary:** Using roughly 535 million employment records across 58 countries, the authors reconstruct the network of AI-skilled worker mobility between firms from 2010 to 2022 and compare it to general labor mobility patterns. They find AI talent inflows are increasingly concentrated among a small set of leading firms with stronger preferential attachment than ordinary labor markets, even as central network positions change hands unusually often; event-study estimates show that firms whose network position improves sharply subsequently gain enterprise value relative to matched firms with similar pre-event trends. The results indicate that a firm's AI advantage depends not just on how much AI talent it accumulates but on its position in the broader circulation of that talent across the labor market.

## 3. Displacement Is Endogenous: AI Workers in a Search-and-Matching Labor Market [Employment]

**Authors:** Ibrahim Denis Fofanah
**Date:** Submitted 2026-10-10
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14850
**Summary:** The paper builds a search-and-matching labor market model in which AI systems compete directly with humans for jobs, applying, passing screening, getting hired, and getting dismissed, with product demand sloping downward. Across simulated configurations, AI entering at 70% of the human wage raises unemployment by 4.1 percentage points and leaves 12.2 fewer humans employed, with the loss split between layoffs and reduced hiring, while at wage parity (no layoffs at all) the unemployment effect is actually largest because cheap-enough AI induces firms to expand output and hiring together. The key finding is that the size of AI-driven displacement is governed by demand elasticity and the AI-human price gap rather than by screening rules, which only reallocate who gets hired without changing the total jobs lost.

## 4. What Jobs Can AI Learn? Measuring Exposure by Reinforcement Learning [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Philip Moreira Tomei, Bouke Klein Teeselink
**Date:** first posted 2026-05-04; revised 2026-09-27
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.02598
**Summary:** Arguing that existing AI exposure indices measure overlap between current AI capabilities and job tasks rather than which tasks AI can actually learn to perform, the authors score all 17,951 O*NET tasks for reinforcement-learning training feasibility using LLM annotators guided by a rubric built with RL experts and validated against confirmed deployment cases, aggregating this into an occupation-level RL Feasibility Index. The index diverges sharply from conventional exposure measures for specific groups: power plant operators, railroad conductors, and cargo handling supervisors score high on learnability despite low general AI exposure, while creative and interpersonal roles like musicians, physicians, and science managers show the opposite pattern. Because the two measures identify different occupations as AI-exposed, the choice of exposure metric carries direct consequences for which workers policy interventions should target.

## 5. The safe harbor paradox: when combining human and AI skills creates or destroys value [Firm Adoption]

**Authors:** Riccardo Zanardelli
**Date:** first posted 2025-09-17; revised 2026-09-28
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.14057
**Summary:** Challenging the assumption that human oversight reliably mitigates the risks of AI deployment, this paper models the economic value of human-AI skill combinations and shows the policy is high-variance: it maximizes value when genuine augmentation is achieved but destroys value when it is not, a result that is robust even when machine skills uniformly outperform human skills and that is not resolved by improving AI's cost-effectiveness over time. Using Monte Carlo simulation, the author separates augmentation into routing work between human and machine at each task and the synergy a workflow adds, and identifies the cost structure and error-cost regime as the key drivers of when combining skills backfires. The findings imply that blanket human-oversight mandates can create false security, and that firms deploying human-AI teams need deliberate workflow design rather than assuming oversight alone adds value.
