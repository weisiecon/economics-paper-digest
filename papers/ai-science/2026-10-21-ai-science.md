# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-10-21

## 1. Opening Knowledge Gaps Drives Scientific Progress [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Kara Kedrick, Wenlong Yang, Thomas Gebhart, Yang Wang, Russell J. Funk
**Date:** October 19, 2026 (v2; first posted September 26, 2025)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21899
**Summary:** The authors argue that some of the most generative moments in science occur when new conceptual connections create "knowledge gaps" — missing links that complete cycles in an evolving network of scientific concepts — and they build a computational-topology method to detect these gaps at scale across tens of millions of papers from 1900–2020. Gap-opening papers draw fewer citations in their first five years but substantially more over the following two decades, and are more disruptive of prior research, than papers that merely introduce new concept pairs without opening a gap, with the pattern replicating across multiple databases and patent records. The result implies science funders and evaluators should expect, and allow time for, genuinely novel conceptual recombinations to pay off only after a delay.

## 2. Weakly Supervised Adaptation of Scientific Embeddings for Exploratory Literature Mapping [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Roman Neruda, Petra Vidnerová, Jiří Šejnoha, Jakub Mihálik, Tomáš Marvan
**Date:** October 18, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25413
**Summary:** The authors test whether lightly adapting an off-the-shelf scientific embedding model (SPECTER2) to a small, specialized literature corpus can fix a known problem: on narrow fields, similarity scores from general-purpose embeddings compress into an undifferentiated cluster, making it hard to map how papers relate. Using only citation-derived pairs already available in OpenAlex, with no manual labeling, they fine-tune the model on a 704-paper machine-consciousness corpus and show the resulting embeddings spread out more realistically and form more coherent, better-connected topic clusters than the base model or simpler lexical and citation-based alternatives. The authors flag that their evaluation measures internal consistency rather than agreement with independent topical ground truth, positioning the result as an encouraging but preliminary case study for building better AI-assisted literature-mapping tools.

## 3. TRACE: Governing AI-Assisted Research with Principles from Computer Science [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Amirhossein Zohrehvand, Aldona Kapačinskaitė
**Date:** October 16, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24035
**Summary:** Noting that AI now makes it trivial to generate research outputs, the authors adapt control practices from software engineering and security research — version tracking, independent verification, bounded authority, and auditable corrections — into five principles (Track, Review, Accept, Control, Evaluate) for keeping humans in charge of AI-assisted research. They implement these as TRACE-OS, an open-source scaffold of shared instructions and tooling meant to slot into existing lab workflows, pacing AI contributions to the speed of human review rather than the speed of AI generation. The paper frames this as a trust-building standard labs need before they can safely delegate more work to AI and capture the productivity gains it promises.
