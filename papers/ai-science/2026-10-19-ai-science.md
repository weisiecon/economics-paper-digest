# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-10-19

## 1. Faster alone vs. further together: The speed and impact of researchers' responses to societal crises [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Nicholas A. Coles, Joao Francisco Goes Braga Takayanagi, Stephen M. Fiore, Lingfei Wu
**Date:** October 14, 2026 (v2; first posted February 20, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19246
**Summary:** The authors analyze 1.5 million publications from 3.3 million researchers across 225 countries responding to 25 societal crises to study how team size trades off against speed and impact. Solo researchers published faster but received less scholarly, news, and policy attention, while larger teams were more impactful but with diminishing returns that varied by crisis and depended on factors like government effectiveness and team-leader expertise. The results offer evidence-based guidance for structuring research teams to balance speed against impact when responding to future crises.

## 2. Safety Research and the Allocation of Frontier Compute [Science Policy]

**Authors:** Li Gan
**Date:** October 9, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14305
**Summary:** This paper models whether AI safety research should be funded by diverting compute from capability-frontier research or from customer-facing services, since each choice carries a different cost: slowing the frontier versus delaying deployment benefits. The author derives a threshold, based on customers' demand capacity and how fast the value of their compute use diminishes, that determines which funding source is cheaper, and shows that customer-funded safety spending should be front-loaded early in the takeoff. A calibrated example finds early customer-funded safety spending cuts the probability of catastrophe from 75% to 51%, nearly matching the best policy found even when slowing capability research is allowed.

## 3. Orchestrating LLM Agents for Scientific Research: A Pilot Study of Multiple Choice Question (MCQ) Generation and Evaluation [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Yuan An
**Date:** October 15, 2026 (v2; first posted February 21, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18891
**Summary:** The author runs a pilot in which a human researcher coordinates multiple LLM agents to extract, generate, and evaluate SAT-style multiple-choice questions from textbooks, using a 24-criterion quality rubric to compare machine-generated items against expert-vetted ones. Generated items scored well on average but never matched experts on strict criterion-by-criterion equivalence, with persistent gaps in cognitive depth, difficulty calibration, and metadata alignment even as surface qualities like grammar stayed strong. The study's broader point concerns the changing nature of research labor itself: the researcher's role shifted from directly authoring content to specifying, orchestrating, verifying, and governing AI agents, pointing to emerging "AI research operations" skills.

## 4. Quantifying research uniqueness with Unbalanced Optimal Transport: Case studies of ten leading universities in neuroscience [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Yu Ota, Takayuki Ogi, Yo Nakawake, Daichi Mochihashi, Amane Koizumi
**Date:** October 13, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17997
**Summary:** The authors propose a citation-free way to measure how distinctive a university's research is: embedding each researcher's abstracts and using unbalanced optimal transport to compute the share of their work that cannot be matched to a random sample of same-field peers. Applied to ten leading neuroscience institutions, all scored well above a reference population on this uniqueness index, with specialized institutes scoring highest and often concentrating their novelty in narrow niches, and the measure proved uncorrelated with citation impact. Because it captures something citations miss, the index offers research funders and evaluators an alternative lens on the direction and originality of research output rather than just its reception.

## 5. Interpreting Paper-to-Patent Citations in AI-Related PMC Articles [Science Policy]

**Authors:** Haowei Li, Yi Bu
**Date:** October 13, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18109
**Summary:** Using large language models to classify roughly 1,544 patent citations found across 713 AI-related PubMed Central articles, the authors examine how and why papers cite patents, distinguishing background references from citations tied to actual technology adoption. Adoption-oriented citations were more associated with methodological framing and favorable evaluation, while background citations tended to be neutral scene-setting, and papers that cited patents received fewer scholarly citations but more citations from other patents. The findings caution that paper-to-patent citations are a selective, heterogeneous signal of science-technology engagement rather than a clean measure of knowledge transfer, with implications for how innovation-policy analysts use such citation counts.
