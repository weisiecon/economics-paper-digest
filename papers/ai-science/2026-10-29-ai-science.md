# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-10-29

## 1. GenAI-Assisted Writing and Academic Performance: Evidence from the Social and Behavioral Sciences [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Dragan Filimonovic, Christian Rutzer, Conny Wunsch
**Date:** October 2, 2025 (updated October 23, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.02408
**Summary:** Using a difference-in-differences design that flags authors as GenAI-assisted writers once ChatGPT-era wording patterns spike in their titles and abstracts, the authors estimate that these writers' publication output rose by roughly 72% relative to matched peers in the social and behavioral sciences. Journal placement barely moved on average, though the very best papers in a GenAI-assisted author's annual portfolio placed meaningfully higher, an effect that largely disappears once total publication count is held fixed. The productivity gains were largest for early-career researchers and authors writing in a language linguistically distant from English, suggesting GenAI writing tools are lowering language and experience barriers to publishing rather than simply inflating volume.

## 2. More of the same? Are scientific papers losing originality? [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Ilan Doron-Arad, Elchanan Mossel
**Date:** September 26, 2026 (updated October 27, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32963
**Summary:** Screening more than 26,000 full-text papers across eight fields for passages that semantically echo recent literature, the authors find that textual and idea-level resemblance to prior work was flat before 2021 and then more than tripled by 2025. The rise is not driven by a few papers borrowing heavily; instead, resemblance to recent work has spread broadly across the literature, and almost none of it is explained by citations. The pattern survived robustness checks and replicated in an independent database, suggesting that along this measurable dimension, published science is becoming less original.

## 3. Officially corrected errors persist in AI training data: preregistered measurements on EU law, Internet standards and retracted science [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Sébastien Gracia
**Date:** October 25, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34379
**Summary:** In a preregistered audit, the author checks whether corrections officially issued after publication — EU legal corrigenda, RFC errata, and paper retractions — actually disappear from major LLM training corpora, finding that over 95% of testable EU-law corrections remain in their erroneous form and that roughly 12,200 retracted papers sit uncorrected in one widely used corpus. When asked directly, several LLMs reproduce the erroneous version in nearly half of their committed answers, though when shown both versions side by side with explicit dates, larger models reliably pick the corrected one. The study shows that the mechanisms meant to keep the scientific and legal record self-correcting are not reliably propagating into the AI systems now built on top of that record.

## 4. What if automating AI R&D triggers an intelligence explosion? [Science Policy]

**Authors:** Alan Chan, Christoph Winter, Andrew Barto, Jakub Pachocki, Geoffrey Hinton, Eric Horvitz, Yoshua Bengio, Dawn Song, Jack Clark, Hilary Greaves, Anton Korinek, Samuel Hammond, Thore Graepel, Ben Bariach, Philip H. S. Torr, Sheila A. McIlraith, Jeff Clune, Sam Manning, Girish Sastry, Tom Davidson, Daniel Eth, Sören Mindermann
**Date:** September 28, 2026 (updated October 26, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.36054
**Summary:** A large group of AI researchers and policy scholars assesses the evidence that AI is close to automating most of the AI research and development pipeline itself, and lays out what could follow if that automation compounds into a rapid, self-reinforcing "intelligence explosion." They argue the preliminary evidence for fast R&D automation is strong enough to warrant urgent attention, since such a feedback loop could compress years of capability gains into months while outpacing society's and governments' ability to adapt or retain control. The paper calls on policymakers to gain better visibility into how much of AI R&D is already automated, build tools to steer or slow the process, and prepare institutions for the disruption an intelligence explosion would cause.

## 5. Why and How to Do Science When AI Is Better at It: A Framework Grounded in Human Understanding and Direction [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Hoda Heidari, Aydin Mohseni
**Date:** October 26, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36116
**Summary:** The authors argue that deciding how researchers should use AI requires first settling why humans should keep doing science at all, and they ground that answer in science's role as a source of durable, collective human understanding pursued for human ends. From this they derive a two-part test for delegating a research task to AI: the community must still be able to explain and contest the resulting claims, and accountable humans must still decide which questions matter and what the findings mean. Applying the test across the stages of research in the CRediT taxonomy — including peer review and the training of new scientists — yields concrete, field-adaptable guidance that holds even if AI eventually outperforms human researchers at every task.
