# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-10-14

## 1. When Scientific Cognition Is No Longer Scarce [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Nathan DeBardeleben
**Date:** October 7, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10241
**Summary:** The author argues that as AI systems become cheaper and more capable than humans at computational scientific work (literature synthesis, hypothesis generation, coding, simulation, analysis), the binding constraint on scientific progress shifts away from cognitive labor toward scarcer resources such as physical experiments, data access, validation, and institutional authority — a "scarcity inversion." This shift is already visible in mathematics and software-centric fields where the entire research loop can run inside a computer, and it poses distinct challenges for institutions like national laboratories, whose role is converting abundant machine reasoning into trustworthy, accountable results. The paper calls for rethinking facility access, verification processes, and scientific governance for a world in which reasoning is plentiful but trustworthy evidence remains scarce.

## 2. University as catalyst of public R&D expenditures? An empirical assessment on EU NUTS 3 regions [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Silvia Iossa, Saverio Barabuffi
**Date:** October 6, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08004
**Summary:** Using panel data on 1,166 EU regions from 2007–2020 and a control-function approach to address endogeneity, the authors test whether the presence of a university amplifies the payoff of public R&D spending on regional patenting. They find public R&D funding boosts patenting in both university and non-university regions with no statistically significant average difference between them, but university presence does help more in regions at risk of, or already experiencing, persistent economic stagnation. The results suggest universities act as a contingent rather than universal multiplier of research funding, with implications for how science funding should be geographically targeted.

## 3. Comprehension Audits to Mitigate Risks from Automated AI Research [Science Policy]

**Authors:** Ronald J. Bodkin, Bahrad A. Sokhansanj, Gillian K. Hadfield
**Date:** October 7, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10064
**Summary:** Noting that AI already writes most of the code at frontier AI labs, the authors propose "comprehension audits" — a governance mechanism requiring the humans responsible for an R&D contribution to demonstrate to independent auditors that they genuinely understand what they built, as a precondition for continuing development. Analyzing open-source AI projects, they document that growing automated code output is accompanied by declining human review commentary per line, especially for fully automated contributions, which they read as evidence of eroding human oversight. They argue labs should adopt such audits, with escalating consequences for repeated failures, to keep increasingly automated research accountable.

## 4. Cooperative Profiles Predict Multi-Agent LLM Team Performance in AI for Science Workflows [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Shivani Kumar, Adarsh Bharathwaj, David Jurgens
**Date:** October 6, 2026 (v2; first posted April 22, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.20658
**Summary:** The authors run 41 open-weight LLMs through six behavioral-economics games designed to isolate cooperation styles, then test whether those "cooperative profiles" predict how the same models perform as multi-agent teams on realistic AI-for-science tasks, such as collaboratively analyzing data and writing scientific reports under shared compute budgets. Models that cooperate well and invest in joint production rather than acting greedily in the games also produce more accurate, higher-quality, and more complete scientific reports as teams, even after controlling for general model ability. The result suggests cooperative disposition is a distinct, measurable trait that labs could use as a cheap pre-deployment screen before committing to costly multi-agent scientific-AI systems.

## 5. SAIL: Scientific Agentic Intelligence via a Science-Aware Loop [AI for Science]

**Authors:** SAIL Model Team, Boyuan Sun, Bryan Dai, Che Liu, Chi Liu, Derek Li, Hongming Piao, Mengzhuo Chen, Xidong Wang, Yan Shu, Yinda Chen, Ziyang Zeng
**Date:** October 8, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11451
**Summary:** The SAIL team introduces an open model with 35B total and 3B active parameters built specifically for literature research, scientific coding, and multi-step research workflows, trained through a "science-aware improvement loop" in which AI agents diagnose the model's own task failures and generate new targeted training problems from real papers and code repositories. Development proceeded through repeated cycles of supervised fine-tuning, specialist training, multi-teacher distillation, and agentic reinforcement learning aimed at closing gaps in literature search, scientific reasoning, and research planning. The model reportedly matches larger open-weight competitors on scientific-research benchmarks despite its much smaller active parameter count, pointing to efficiency gains available for AI-for-science tooling.
