# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-09-23

## 1. Designing AI-Augmented Peer Review [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Joshua S. Gans
**Date:** August 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35688 (NBER Working Paper 35688)
**Summary:** Gans models how journals should fold AI-generated manuscript assessments into peer review, focusing on whether the same AI report should go to every reviewer on a paper or be restricted to just one. He finds that broadly sharing one AI assessment risks reviewers converging on the same blind spots and missing independent problems, so limiting the report to a single reviewer better combines AI assistance with genuine human independence. The paper works through the incentive issues journals face in adopting such a policy — reviewer effort, compliance, and confidentiality — and proposes testing sharing rules by randomizing exposure and measuring reviewer disagreement.

## 2. Social Influence and the Allocation of Scientific Attention in AI Populations [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Maxim Chupilkin
**Date:** September 18, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22408
**Summary:** The author runs a Music-Lab-style experiment in which populations of AI agents choose which economics papers to read from titles and abstracts, comparing communities that choose independently to ones where agents can see what others already picked. When agents observe prior choices, the population reads fewer papers per agent, concentrates attention on a narrower set of titles, and varies more from one community to the next than under independent choice. Because these agents' aggregate picks only weakly track real citation and download patterns, the paper argues that simple exposure to popularity signals could meaningfully distort how AI systems assess and route attention toward scientific work as AI increasingly mediates literature discovery.

## 3. The illusion of neutrality in metric-based research evaluation [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Fengyuan Liu, Hazem Ibrahim, Yasir Zaki, Talal Rahwan
**Date:** September 21, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24471
**Summary:** The authors surveyed nearly 900 researchers with a discrete-choice exercise asking how much weight hypothetical hiring rules should place on venue prestige versus citation counts. On average respondents split their weight almost evenly between the two signals, but that aggregate balance concealed sharp disagreement: roughly two in five researchers leaned heavily toward one signal or the other, and people generally favored more citation-weighted rules than they believed their own departments actually used. The findings undercut the idea that quantitative research metrics offer a neutral standard, since the apparent "balance" only shows up at the population level and hides real variation in how individuals judge research quality.

## 4. Shifting Research Funding Priorities under Geopolitical Pressure: Evidence from Estonia [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Yunfeng Gao, Yang Ding
**Date:** September 16, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18571
**Summary:** Using nearly 18,000 Estonian research-funding records from 2000–2019 linked to OpenAlex publication data, the authors test whether the 2014 Russia-Ukraine conflict shifted the technical orientation of funded science in a neighboring, non-belligerent country. They find that after 2014, funded projects — especially larger ones — moved toward dual-use and higher-capability technical language in fields like computer science, energy, and engineering, even without a rise in explicit war-related terminology. The paper shows that geopolitical pressure can reshape a country's science-funding priorities in ways visible only through fine-grained textual analysis of project language, not through standard field classifications.

## 5. Evaluating Decision Models for Text Annotation in Computational Social Science [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Hazem Ibrahim, Yasir Zaki
**Date:** September 21, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24574
**Summary:** The authors benchmark a new class of "decision models" — cheap, purpose-built classifiers that output a label, a probability distribution, and a confidence score — against 19 general-purpose language models on 18 real computational social science annotation tasks. The decision model underperforms the best LLM on nearly every task and is worse calibrated than several frontier models, but it is dramatically cheaper, and its labels are usually accurate when its stated confidence is high. The authors conclude decision models work best as a triage step that routes only low-confidence items to a full LLM, cutting annotation costs while preserving most of the accuracy in AI-assisted research pipelines.
