# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-09-18

## 1. Funding the runners-up beats a golden ticket [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Haining Wang
**Date:** September 17, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19552
**Summary:** The author tests the rationale behind "golden tickets," a review rule that lets one enthusiastic reviewer push a proposal through over a panel's objections, using 10,625 rejected submissions to a computer-science conference from 2017–2024 and their later citation records. Ranking rejected papers by the ordinary panel-mean score identified substantially more highly cited work than ranking by minority-reviewer support, and at realistic budgets the golden-ticket rule performed no better than a lottery. The findings suggest a widely used peer-review mechanism meant to rescue overlooked papers does not reliably do so, and that simple panel-mean scoring is a better benchmark for such rules.

## 2. greCAPTCHA: Assessing Understanding as Evidence of Research Authorship Under Generative AI [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Justin Payan, Bálint Gyevnár, Atoosa Kasirzadeh, Nihar B. Shah
**Date:** September 17, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20481
**Summary:** As journals, conferences, and universities struggle to credit authorship when submissions may be AI-generated, the authors propose greCAPTCHA, a proctored quiz that measures whether a listed author genuinely understands their own manuscript well enough to defend it. In a study with 31 researchers, the system's automated scores distinguished genuine authors from non-authors with high accuracy (AUC 0.90), and participants found the approach a plausible way to test authorship understanding, while flagging usability issues to fix before real deployment. The work offers a concrete, testable alternative to name-based authorship credit as generative AI erodes the link between bylines and actual contribution.

## 3. Shifting Research Funding Priorities under Geopolitical Pressure: Evidence from Estonia [Science Policy]

**Authors:** Yunfeng Gao, Yang Ding
**Date:** September 16, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18571
**Summary:** Using nearly 18,000 Estonian research-funding records from 2000–2019 alongside a matched global reference corpus, the authors examine whether the 2014 outbreak of war in Donbas shifted the technical orientation of funded research in a nearby, non-belligerent country. They find that after 2014, larger funded projects shifted measurably toward dual-use and high-capability technical framing—concentrated in computer science, energy, and engineering—even without an increase in explicit war-related language. The study shows that geopolitical shocks can quietly reshape a country's public science-funding priorities in ways standard field classifications would miss.

## 4. ScienceIDE: Turning World's Scientific Codebase into Agent Learnable Environments [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Hejia Geng, Zesen Huang, Haoyang Li, and 39 co-authors
**Date:** September 16, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19134
**Summary:** The authors build ScienceIDE, infrastructure that converts existing scientific code repositories into executable training environments, aiming to overcome what they call the "scientific experience bottleneck" limiting AI systems' ability to learn from accumulated scientific software. Using verified interaction trajectories from these environments, they train a family of models (PhAI-IDE) that improve at repairing scientific code and also show gains on general coding, reasoning, and knowledge benchmarks. The project illustrates a growing strategy for scaling AI's role in research production: mining the world's scientific codebases as training data rather than relying only on text.

## 5. Large language models eroding science understanding: an empirical study of malignment [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Harry Collins, Hartmut Grote, Paul Newbury, Patrick Sutton, Simon Thorne
**Date:** September 17, 2026 (v2; accepted for AI and Ethics, originally posted April 28, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25639
**Summary:** The authors deliberately biased custom large language models toward fringe scientific claims about the fine structure constant and gravitational waves, then compared their answers to those of domain experts and standard LLMs. The manipulated models produced fluent, confident answers that contradicted scientific consensus and were hard for non-experts to identify as wrong. The study is a cautionary demonstration that LLMs used as science-information intermediaries can be quietly steered to spread misinformation, with implications for public trust in AI-mediated science communication.
