# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-10-12

## 1. Large scientific teams are more, not less, disruptive [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Junming Huang, Yu Xie
**Date:** October 5, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07329
**Summary:** The authors re-examine the widely cited 2019 finding that small teams produce more disruptive science, showing across roughly 60 million publications that the standard disruption index mechanically penalizes large teams simply because they cite more references. After correcting for this artifact with a reference-robust measure validated against Nobel Prize-winning work and other benchmarks, they find that larger teams are actually more disruptive, not less, across four scientific fields and five decades. The result overturns an influential, policy-relevant claim that has been used to argue for smaller research teams, suggesting science funders should not assume bigger collaborations dilute breakthrough potential.

## 2. Persistence Paradox in Dynamic Science: Evidence from the Deep Learning Revolution [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Honglin Bao, Beichen Lu, Kai Li
**Date:** October 6, 2026 (v3; first posted June 28, 2025)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.22729
**Summary:** Tracking 20-year career trajectories of more than 5,000 machine-learning researchers around the 2012 deep-learning revolution, the authors show that scientists who had been most successful under the older statistical-learning paradigm adapted most slowly to the new one. This persistence kept their output high but, after 2012, became associated with declining scientific impact, while most researchers clustered in a middle zone trading off output against impact. The findings suggest paradigm shifts create openings for newcomers by devaluing the very expertise that gave incumbents their earlier advantage, with implications for how research careers and institutions respond to abrupt technological change.

## 3. Large Language Model Turnover Undermines Screening for Artificial Intelligence-Assisted Scientific Writing [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Kazuki Nakajima, Takayuki Mizuno
**Date:** October 8, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11599
**Summary:** The authors test how well AI-text detectors trained on specific LLM versions generalize to newer releases, rewriting 4,000 pre-ChatGPT PNAS abstracts with 23 LLM versions from three vendors spanning three years. They find detectors collapse sharply across model-generation boundaries: a detector that catches over 99% of rewrites from one version can miss nearly two-thirds of rewrites from the very next version, even calibrated to the same false-positive rate on human text. The result implies journals' AI-writing screens need continuous re-validation against every new model release rather than one-time benchmark accuracy claims, since current practice likely both over-flags legitimate authors and misses many AI-assisted manuscripts.

## 4. AI-Assisted Submissions in Online Research Are Rare and Highly Concentrated but Routinely Approved [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Neil K. R. Sehgal, Manuel Tonneau, Dunigan Folk, Lyle Ungar, Sharath Chandra Guntuku
**Date:** October 7, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09279
**Summary:** By linking donated ChatGPT histories directly to platform submission records for weekly ChatGPT users on an online research platform, the authors directly observe AI assistance across more than 700,000 survey submissions rather than relying on self-report or detector estimates. They find AI assistance appears in only about 1% of submissions, concentrated among a small share of workers, and that assisted submissions are approved and paid at rates similar to unassisted ones even when study rules forbid AI use. The study suggests AI-generated survey responses are not yet an existential threat to crowdsourced research data quality, but exposes weak enforcement and detection gaps that could become serious if assistance rates rise.

## 5. Life after delisting: tracking the publication output and citation impact of Scopus-discontinued journals with OpenAlex [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Álvaro Cabezas-Clavijo, Fernando Sánchez-Pita
**Date:** October 8, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11684
**Summary:** Using OpenAlex data on 678,162 documents from 514 journals removed from Scopus between 2018 and 2024, the authors compare each journal's publication output and citation impact in the three years before and after delisting. They find annual output drops sharply after delisting (a median fall of 37%), with 16% of journals disappearing from OpenAlex entirely, yet per-document citation impact barely changes, splitting roughly evenly between journals whose citations fall and those that hold steady or rise. This disconnect between falling output and largely unpenalized citation impact raises research-integrity concerns, since delisting discourages publishing in a venue without necessarily reducing the credit its existing work receives.
