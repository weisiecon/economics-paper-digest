# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-11-18

## 1. Same Papers, Different Verdicts: Tracing Version Drift in LLM Peer Review [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Jizhou Guo
**Date:** November 16, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20608
**Summary:** The author audits twenty frontier LLMs from OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic as peer reviewers on ICLR 2027 submissions, using pre-registered tests to compare how successive model versions score the same papers. Switching from an earlier to a later version of Claude Opus or Gemini shifts a paper's accept/reject verdict by 40-80 percentage points even when no paper moves toward acceptance, and swapping only the review text (not the score) between versions reproduces much of this drift. The results show that LLM-assigned peer-review scores are measurements of the model version as much as of the paper, so any acceptance threshold set on one model generation cannot be assumed valid for the next.

## 2. Nonlinear Journal Prestige Normalized Productivity: Defining and Measuring It [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Marek Kwiek
**Date:** November 13, 2026 (v2; originally posted July 18, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22701
**Summary:** Kwiek proposes a new productivity indicator, Journal Prestige Normalized Productivity (JPNP), that combines percentile-based journal prestige rankings, a convex transformation emphasizing top-tier venues, and fractional authorship credit, rather than simply counting publications. Applied at the level of disciplines, institutions, and countries, JPNP yields a more skewed but more realistic picture of who is driving scientific output than linear publication-count measures, and better isolates top performers while discounting low-prestige venues. The author argues the indicator is better suited than existing metrics for research evaluation, institutional benchmarking, and science policy because it tracks the actual hierarchical structure of global scholarly publishing.

## 3. When AI Improves Answers but Slows Knowledge Creation: Matching and Dynamic Knowledge Creation in Digital Public Goods [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Keh-Kuan Sun
**Date:** November 13, 2026 (v2; originally posted April 1, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00468
**Summary:** Sun builds a dynamic model of a public question-and-answer platform in which generative AI can divert users toward private chatbot conversations instead of posting questions publicly, and can also discourage contributors from answering because fewer people remain to benefit. Even though AI improves the quality of individual answers, it can shrink the platform's shared knowledge archive through these two channels, and can push platforms that were already fragile into a self-reinforcing "low-archive trap" where public answering becomes too costly to sustain. The model generates testable predictions for distinguishing AI-driven demand-side diversion from supply-side contributor exit, and suggests that logging private AI interactions or rewarding public answers could help preserve reusable public knowledge.

## 4. Comparison of OpenAlex and Scopus coverage of German institutions' publications in top-tier journals [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Andrey Lovakov, Ivan Sterligov
**Date:** November 16, 2026 (v2; originally posted May 2, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.01337
**Summary:** Lovakov and Sterligov compare how the open bibliometric database OpenAlex and the proprietary database Scopus count publications by German research institutions in top-tier journals, focusing specifically on the quality of institutional affiliation metadata. OpenAlex indexes more papers overall but attributes about 6.8% fewer institution-paper links than Scopus, with discrepancies concentrated in specific fields, publishers, and journals due to missing affiliations and differing institutional boundary definitions; aggregate institutional rankings nevertheless correlate highly between the two sources. The authors conclude OpenAlex is reliable for comparative institutional analysis but still needs better affiliation data before it can substitute for Scopus in evaluations that depend on absolute publication counts.

## 5. Efficiency Gains in Social Science Research: A Prospective Silicon Sampling Protocol for Pre-Treatment and Code Optimization [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Noaïm Astitou, Etienne Aucouturier
**Date:** November 13, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18436
**Summary:** The authors test whether generating synthetic survey responses with LLMs (Claude, GPT, and Kimi) before real data collection can speed up the preparation of data-cleaning and analysis code for a real survey in the France Life Imaging network. Using synthetic data this way let researchers reuse 85-90% of their analysis code once real responses arrived, but the approach worked only for predictable, general topics and struggled to anticipate specialized or highly contextual responses, consistent with known homogenization limits of LLM-simulated respondents. The study extends prior "Phase 0" synthetic-data validation work into a practical preprocessing step for survey researchers, trading some fidelity for substantial time savings in code development.
