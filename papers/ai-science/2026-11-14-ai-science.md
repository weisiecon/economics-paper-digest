# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-11-14

## 1. Auditing AI peer reviewers: a benchmark on real scientific papers [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Íñigo Zubeldia, Boris Bolliet, Francisco Villaescusa-Navarro, Pablo Villanueva-Domingo, Miles Cranmer
**Date:** November 12, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17052
**Summary:** The authors build a benchmark that plants 357 objective, localizable errors (general issues, math, numerics, citations) at varying severity into real astrophysics and machine-learning preprints, then score how well twelve AI reviewer systems catch them. Which underlying LLM a reviewer uses matters more than any tool or scaffolding, with top frontier models differing by about 50 percentage points in detection; their own multi-agent reviewer, which verifies citations by retrieving the cited papers and checks math and numbers with external tools, wins clearly on citation errors in astrophysics but not in machine learning, where models already know the literature well. False-positive rates stay under 10% for most systems, with spurious math flags the main culprit.

## 2. Unequal Rewards and the Direction of Scientific Work: Evidence from U.S. Public Universities [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Likun Cao
**Date:** November 12, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16747
**Summary:** Using payroll and publication records for over 105,000 tenure-line faculty across nearly 4,800 departments at 93 U.S. public universities (2004–2026), the author isolates within-department pay dispersion that rank, seniority, and contract type cannot explain and asks what kind of research it rewards. Scholars in more unequally paid departments publish more and get cited more, but their work is less novel and less disruptive, while above-ladder pay premiums flow disproportionately to researchers with broad collaborations and topical range. The findings suggest unequal pay functions as an evaluative structure that steers scientific effort toward legible, incremental contributions rather than just a distributional outcome.

## 3. More Papers, Not More Output: Collaboration and Publication Trends among Early-Career Researchers [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Christian Leibel
**Date:** November 9, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12571
**Summary:** Drawing on publication records for more than 200,000 early-career researchers across five science, engineering, and health fields from 1996 to 2024, the author tests whether rising paper counts reflect more individual output or just larger teams. Recent cohorts appear on more papers than earlier ones, but team sizes have grown so much that collaboration-adjusted output (credit divided among coauthors) has stayed flat or fallen in every field studied. The result challenges the common use of raw publication counts in hiring and promotion, since they increasingly diverge from how much any one researcher actually contributes.

## 4. Earlier citation practices are linked to signs of artificial intelligence use in scientific writing [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Hongkan Chen, Yongyuan He, Qingshan Zhou, Yi Bu
**Date:** November 12, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16833
**Summary:** Analyzing OpenAlex publication histories for over 71,000 authors with at least one eligible paper every year from 2023 through September 2026, the authors measure "resistance" to AI-assisted writing as the gap between an author's predicted and observed share of likely AI-assisted abstracts. Authors who showed less AI-assistance signal than expected across 2023–2026 tended to cite older, narrower, and more self-repeated reference sets, and this citation pattern was already visible before ChatGPT's release, with the gap in reference reuse widening over time. The results suggest that how scientists already engaged with the literature, not just whether they adopted new tools, predicts observable shifts in writing style after AI became available.

## 5. Can AI be a 'Researcher Without Borders'? Auditing Bias in AI-Assisted Social Science Research about China and the US [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Yuan Zhang, Thales Bertaglia, Ralph Schroeder
**Date:** November 10, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14288
**Summary:** The authors have five LLMs (two U.S.-developed, two China-developed, one European) write short essays on social science questions about the US and China, varying the question type and the researcher identity the model is asked to adopt, then have human raters code the outputs for bias and source use. Models mostly avoided clear political bias toward their country of origin, but differed systematically in which sources they cited, and some comparative questions still favored one country over the other. The authors conclude AI can plausibly act as an impartial research assistant on cross-national questions but that researchers should still check for subtler sourcing asymmetries.
