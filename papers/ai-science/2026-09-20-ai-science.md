# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-09-20

## 1. greCAPTCHA: Assessing Understanding as Evidence of Research Authorship Under Generative AI [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Justin Payan, Bálint Gyevnár, Atoosa Kasirzadeh, Nihar B. Shah
**Date:** September 17, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20481
**Summary:** With institutions no longer able to trust that a listed author actually understands their own AI-assisted manuscript, the authors build greCAPTCHA, a proctored assessment that quizzes authors on their manuscript to gauge whether they can verify and defend its content. In a study with 31 researchers, the system's automated understanding scores predicted whether a participant had actually authored a given paper with an AUC of 0.90, and participants generally found the experience reasonable despite flagging changes needed before real deployment. The approach offers conferences, journals, and funders a concrete tool for authenticating authorship and human-oversight claims in an era of generative-AI-assisted writing.

## 2. Funding the runners-up beats a golden ticket [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Haining Wang
**Date:** September 17, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19552
**Summary:** Using 10,625 rejected submissions to a computer-science conference from 2017-2024, the author tests whether "golden tickets" — letting one reviewer override a panel's rejection — actually rescue undervalued work, comparing this minority-support rule against simpler allocation rules using later citation outcomes. At a budget of 10% of the year's accepted papers, ranking by panel-mean score selected submissions with citation percentiles nearly 8 points higher than minority-support selection, which performed little better than a lottery. The findings challenge the rationale for reviewer-override mechanisms in peer review and suggest panel consensus is a stronger signal of a rejected paper's eventual impact than a single dissenting champion.

## 3. Shifting Research Funding Priorities under Geopolitical Pressure: Evidence from Estonia [Science Policy]

**Authors:** Yunfeng Gao, Yang Ding
**Date:** September 16, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18571
**Summary:** Combining nearly 18,000 Estonian research-funding records from 2000-2019 with a matched OpenAlex reference corpus and geocoded Donbas conflict data, the authors ask whether a geopolitically exposed but non-belligerent country shifted its science funding after the 2014 war broke out in Ukraine. They find that after 2014 Estonia's funded projects moved toward dual-use and general-capability-oriented language rather than explicit war terminology, with the shift concentrated in larger grants and strongest in computer science, energy, and engineering. The study shows that geopolitical pressure can quietly reshape a country's public science-funding priorities in ways standard field classifications would miss.

## 4. Large language models eroding science understanding: an empirical study of malignment [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Harry Collins, Hartmut Grote, Paul Newbury, Patrick Sutton, Simon Thorne
**Date:** September 17, 2026 (v2; originally posted April 28, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25639
**Summary:** The authors deliberately fine-tuned custom LLMs to favor fringe scientific claims about the fine structure constant and gravitational waves, then compared their answers on those topics against domain experts and standard LLMs. The manipulated models produced fluent, confident answers that contradicted scientific consensus and were hard for non-experts to distinguish from legitimate ones. The results, now accepted for publication in AI and Ethics, illustrate a concrete channel by which LLMs could be misused to erode public and non-expert understanding of settled science.

## 5. ScienceIDE: Turning World's Scientific Codebase into Agent Learnable Environments [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Hejia Geng, Zesen Huang, Haoyang Li, Wenbin Li, Koutian Wu, Zihan Zhou, Yuanbo Pang, Weihao Liu, Zigong Xu, Zhiping Li, Zongzheng Zhang, Chuanfei Dong, Jiankai Sun, Tianzhe Zheng, Fengyu Xie, Yue Ma, Yueheng Shi, Tong Xie, Zonglin Di, Xianrong Liu, Qucheng Gao, Yimin Liu, Jiaming Pan, Sheng Huang, Xiao-Han Ma, Lanqing Yuan, Zhenlin Zhu, Ziang Liu, Ziyang Xu, Junkai Wang, Kangkai Liang, Jiayi Xian, Zehong Zhao, Liuwei Xu, Jingxu Xie, Peijin Zhang, Qiang Gao, Chengyi Xing, Zhe Zhao, Xi Wang, Yaopeng Xing, Xing Meng, Zhenfei Yin, Yingcheng Wu, Ling Yang
**Date:** September 16, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19134
**Summary:** The authors build ScienceIDE, infrastructure that converts scientific code repositories into executable, verifiable training environments for AI agents, addressing what they call a "scientific experience bottleneck" caused by fragmented toolchains and domain-specific correctness criteria. Using verified interaction trajectories generated from these environments, they train a family of models (PhAI-IDE) that show gains on held-out scientific-code repair and transfer improvements to general-purpose coding, reasoning, and knowledge benchmarks. The work points toward treating the world's existing scientific software as reusable training data for building more capable AI research agents.
