# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-11-11

## 1. More Papers, Not More Output: Collaboration and Publication Trends among Early-Career Researchers [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Christian Leibel
**Date:** November 9, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12571
**Summary:** Using publication records for more than 200,000 early-career researchers across five science, engineering, and health fields from 1996 to 2024, the author tests whether rising paper counts reflect genuine gains in individual output or simply larger co-authorship teams. Recent cohorts do appear on more papers, but they also work in much bigger teams, so once publication credit is divided among coauthors, their collaboration-adjusted output is flat or declining in every field studied. The finding undercuts a common assumption behind hiring and tenure metrics: raw publication counts increasingly overstate how much any individual researcher is actually producing.

## 2. Winning Reviews, Losing Discovery? The Co-Evolutionary Game of AI Research and Peer Review [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Hongze Liu, Mengru Wang, Zhiyuan Zhang, Yao Liu
**Date:** November 7, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10791
**Summary:** The authors build a two-population evolutionary game in which authors choose between acceptance-oriented and exploratory research while reviewers choose between cheap proxy evaluation and careful assessment, then derive the conditions under which cheaper AI-assisted manuscript production and lighter-touch AI-assisted reviewing reinforce each other into a stable low-value equilibrium. In that regime, publication counts keep rising even as the scientific value of accepted work falls, and the model shows that partial reforms can fail once the feedback loop is entrenched, while sustained investment in careful audits and reviewer incentives can push the system back across the basin into a discovery-rewarding regime. The paper is a warning about institutional design rather than a prediction for any specific venue, arguing that review systems should reward independently validated contributions rather than just accelerate throughput.

## 3. Known Good, Known Bad: A Ground-Truth Calibration Protocol for AI Judgment in Science [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Olga Lavinda
**Date:** November 8, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11673
**Summary:** The author proposes a calibration protocol for testing generative AI's scientific judgment by comparing model responses against a verified reference case and several deliberately flawed variants, scoring whether the model detects the injected errors, accommodates false premises, raises unsupported doubts, or gets the underlying calculation right. In a pilot built around an organic chemistry demonstration (six conversations, 18 responses from a logged-out ChatGPT session), the model missed two of five intended deviations on a neutral first pass but caught all five once prompted with an authority-framed follow-up, and it sometimes corrected a unit error while still carrying over an now-invalid numeric conclusion. The protocol is offered as a reusable, inspectable template for calibrating AI-assisted scientific evaluation rather than a general verdict on model reliability, with the author flagging replication across more cases as the necessary next step.
