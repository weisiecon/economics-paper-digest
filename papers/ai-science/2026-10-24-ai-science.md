# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-10-24

## 1. Major Revision, Minor Accountability: LLMs as Reviewers of Published Articles in Top-Tier Journals [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Bastien Le Guellec, Su Hwan Kim, Raphael Bentegeac, Aghiles Hamroun
**Date:** October 22, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31414
**Summary:** The authors had four current LLMs blindly peer-review 200 already-published articles from top journals like Nature, Science, and Cell, then repeated the exercise after planting a deliberate methodological flaw in each manuscript. The models recommended major revision or rejection for three-quarters of papers that had already cleared real editorial review, yet individual models disagreed wildly with each other and caught the planted flaws only 17-60% of the time depending on the model. The findings suggest that AI-generated peer review verdicts are an inconsistent, model-dependent signal rather than a reliable stand-in for human assessment of scientific validity.

## 2. FLAWS: A Benchmark for Error Identification and Localization in Scientific Papers [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Sarina Xi, Vishisht Rao, Justin Payan, Nihar B. Shah
**Date:** October 22, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.21843
**Summary:** The authors built FLAWS, a benchmark of 713 paper-error pairs created by using LLMs to insert claim-invalidating errors into peer-reviewed papers, to test whether frontier LLMs can find and pinpoint flaws that undermine a paper's central claims. Testing five leading models, including GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Claude Sonnet 4.5, they found that even the best performer identified only about 39% of inserted errors among its top 10 guesses. The results point to a gap between the growing use of LLMs in review pipelines and their actual reliability at the core reviewing task of catching substantive mistakes.

## 3. AI-Native Academia Should Build a Community Verification Layer [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Haotian Zong, Luhao Zhang
**Date:** October 22, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30549
**Summary:** This position paper argues that as AI lowers the cost of producing plausible-looking research faster than the scholarly community can evaluate it, academia needs shared infrastructure that turns routine reading, citing, and replication work into persistent, claim-linked evidence rather than letting it evaporate. The authors propose a tiered "Community Verification Layer" spanning comprehension notes up through audits, reproduction, and downstream validation, with judgments elicited before social signals are revealed and verification labor made citable. They distinguish the proposal from existing open and post-publication review models and discuss the governance challenges of building and incentivizing such a system.

## 4. Stop Drawing Scientific Claims from LLM Social Simulations Without Robustness Audits [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Jinyi Ye, Lei Cao, Ding Chen, Emilio Ferrara
**Date:** October 22, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.18890
**Summary:** The authors show that scientific conclusions drawn from LLM-based social simulations can hinge on arbitrary implementation choices rather than the social mechanisms researchers intend to study: minor changes to agent personas or game framing shifted cooperation rates by up to 76 percentage points in a repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, with sensitivity varying sharply across models. To address this, they introduce TRAILS, a taxonomy for auditing the robustness of LLM simulations at the agent, interaction, and system level, and argue robustness checks should be a mandatory validation step before such simulations inform scientific claims or policy decisions. The paper is a methodological warning for the growing use of generative-agent simulations as a research tool in computational social science.
