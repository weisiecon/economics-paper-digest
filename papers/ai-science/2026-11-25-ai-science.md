# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-11-25

## 1. Survivors in science: A comprehensive analysis of gender disparities [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Marek Kwiek, Lukasz Szymula
**Date:** Updated November 21, 2026 (originally posted December 11, 2025)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.22140
**Summary:** Using Scopus bibliometric data on 41,424 scientists who published continuously from 2000 to 2020-2023 across 38 OECD countries, the authors isolate gender gaps among researchers who remain in science long-term rather than those who exit the pipeline early. They find men were roughly 20% more productive than women on average over the full period, even as men and women published in similarly prestigious journals, achieved comparable citation impact, and worked in similarly sized teams. The results show that gender inequality in science is career-stage dependent: disparities seen across the broader researcher population shrink or disappear among long-term "survivors," except for the persistent productivity gap.

## 2. Smaller, Younger, and More Visible? AI-Assisted Writing, Scientific Team Composition, and the Visibility of Scientific Knowledge [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Mingze Zhang, Haoyang Wang, Yi Bu, Star Xing Zhao, Meijun Liu
**Date:** Updated November 23, 2026 (originally posted April 24, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.27404
**Summary:** Analyzing 144,930 full-text articles from the PLoS family and Nature Portfolio journals published 2020-2025, the authors build a text-based detector to estimate how much AI-assisted writing shaped each paper, then relate that score to the composition of the publishing team and the paper's short-term citation impact. They find papers with higher estimated AI usage tend to come from younger, smaller teams and receive more citations within a year of publication, though the team-size effect is weaker at Nature journals. The findings suggest generative AI may be reshaping who gets heard in science, narrowing the usual visibility advantage that large, established teams enjoy.

## 3. Managing Verification Debt: A Framework for Responsible Human-AI Research Partnerships [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Abdulkadir Celik, Sir Bashir M. Al-Hashimi
**Date:** November 22, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29434
**Summary:** This commentary names a problem it calls "verification debt": the gap between how fast generative AI can produce research ideas and claims, and how slowly human experts can check them, which leaves unexamined AI contributions embedded in later work. The authors propose a shared "topology" for a research programme that tracks each claim's assumptions, sources, and verification status so unresolved debt stays visible and corrections can propagate to dependent claims. They argue this kind of structured tracking, rather than better prompting, is what responsible human-AI research collaboration requires, with implications for how peer review and doctoral supervision should adapt.

## 4. MagTrace: Tracing magnetic order through the evolving scientific literature [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Mengnan Sun, Cheng Peng
**Date:** November 20, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.28338
**Summary:** The authors build MagTrace, a language-model agent that, given just a material's name, searches multiple literature databases and reports what magnetic order the current published record supports, with a confidence score and checkable citations. Tested against a hand-built answer key for 18 materials, MagTrace scores well above both a single-search baseline and the model answering from unaided memory, and it hallucinates far fewer nonexistent or contradicted citations than the memory-only approach. The work illustrates how an AI research agent can make literature synthesis more reliable and auditable, a capability relevant to how science is produced and verified at scale.
