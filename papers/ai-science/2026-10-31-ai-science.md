# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-10-31

## 1. Beyond fabrication: Misattributed numbers in papers written by AI research agents [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Mosab Alfaqeeh, Omar Hourani, Muath Hourani, Hashem Alfaqeeh
**Date:** October 28, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38691
**Summary:** The authors re-examine 540 integrity judgments and manually label 256 numerical claims from AI research agents' papers in a public benchmark, finding that most failures are not outright fabrication but numbers pulled from the agent's own files that belong to the wrong experiment, metric, or run. They build a rule-based checker that verifies whether a claim's surrounding text correctly identifies its supporting evidence, certifying claims with over 90% precision against human annotators, while an LLM-based verifier catches more claims but also accepts roughly three times as many misattributed ones. Applied across 99 AI-written submissions, the checker exposes large differences in how traceable different agents' numerical claims are, arguing for provenance-based auditing before AI-generated papers are trusted.

## 2. More of the same? Are scientific papers losing originality? [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Ilan Doron-Arad, Elchanan Mossel
**Date:** October 27, 2026 (v2; first posted September 26, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32963
**Summary:** Using semantic language models to screen more than 26,000 full-text papers across eight fields from 2015–2025, the authors measure how closely each paper's text resembles a fixed-size sample of earlier work, controlling for the fact that a larger literature alone could inflate apparent resemblance. They find textual resemblance to prior work was flat before 2021 but then more than tripled by 2025, driven not by a few repetitive papers but by the pattern spreading across the literature, with almost none of the increase explained by papers' own citations. The result replicates in an independent database, suggesting scientific writing has grown measurably less distinct from its predecessors — a finding science funders and evaluators may want to track as the literature keeps expanding.

## 3. Behind the Paper Explosion: Author Growth and Widening Gaps in Computer Science [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Jiayi Li, Bowen Niu, Bingsheng He, Zhaomin Wu
**Date:** October 29, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39831
**Summary:** Using arXiv, DBLP, and conference submission records from 2010–2025, the authors decompose the surge in computer science papers into growth in the number of publishing authors versus growth in papers per author, crediting nearly all of the increase to more people publishing rather than existing authors writing more. Tracking the same individuals over time reveals substantial turnover within the growing author pool, and among authors who keep publishing, output gaps are widening even between researchers who previously published similar numbers of papers. The findings bear on how reviewing load, newcomer participation, and equity in researcher evaluation should be read against the field's headline growth numbers.

## 4. Interpreting LLM Scores in Peer Review: Alignment and Divergence Across Prompts, Domains, and Manuscript Features [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Wenqiang Qin, Renlong Jie, Yanjun Qian, Salma Srifi
**Date:** October 26, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35098
**Summary:** The authors compare four LLMs' review scores against human ratings on 7,083 ICLR 2024 submissions, then probe one model across ten prompting variations to see how much scoring behavior depends on instructions rather than manuscript quality. Giving the model area-chair-style guidance sharply cut its average divergence from human scores and nearly halved its per-paper error, while combining historical reference scores with downward-scoring instructions pushed divergence lower still, yet rank correlation with human judgments barely moved and domain-level differences persisted after calibration. The results show LLM "scores" in peer review are highly sensitive to how they're elicited, so any adoption of LLM scoring needs separate evidence for aggregate alignment versus consistent ranking of papers.

## 5. An Analysis of Artificial Intelligence Adoption in NIH-Funded Research [Science Policy]

**Authors:** Navapat Nananukul, Mayank Kejriwal
**Date:** October 29, 2026 (v2; first posted April 8, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.07424
**Summary:** Using an LLM-based pipeline to classify and summarize nearly 59,000 NIH-funded biomedical research project descriptions, the authors map how much of the NIH's funding portfolio involves AI or machine learning and where that funding concentrates. They find AI accounts for about 15.9% of the portfolio and carries a 13.4% funding premium over non-AI projects, concentrated in discovery, prediction, and data-integration work, but 79% of AI-funded projects remain stuck in research and development, with only about 14.7% reaching clinical deployment, and health-disparities research makes up just 5.7% of AI-funded work. The analysis gives funders a baseline for checking whether AI investment is translating into clinical impact and reaching NIH's equity priorities, rather than just accumulating in early-stage research.
