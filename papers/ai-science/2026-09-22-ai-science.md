# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-09-22

## 1. Making AI-Assisted Claims Independently Challengeable: Publication Authority and a Protocol for Falsifiable Publication Records [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Torsten Olivi Tiltack, Yifei Dong, Kun Yu, Xu Wang, Wei Liu, Jianlong Zhou, Ren Ping Liu, Fang Chen
**Date:** September 15, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17631
**Summary:** The authors argue that AI-assisted research claims can look authoritative even when the underlying evidence, analysis, and human sign-off don't actually line up, and that existing provenance and transparency records alone don't catch this. They design "Publication Authority," a formal, single-use permission scheme instantiated in a protocol (PAC-2026) with six required checks spanning evidence, execution artifacts, measurement disclosure, and authorization, then stress-test it computationally across roughly 110,000 possible states plus adversarial and historical cases. The work is infrastructure for machine-checkable publication integrity rather than a policy study, aimed at making it harder for AI-assisted claims to enter the scientific record without a verifiable chain of accountability.

## 2. Rethinking Domain Specialization for Open-Ended Scientific Reasoning in Astronomy Language Models [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Vanessa Lama, Sanjay Das, Emily Herron, Yuan-Sen Ting, Tijmen de Haan, Junqi Yin, Tirthankar Ghosal, Feiyi Wang
**Date:** September 15, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17644
**Summary:** The authors built a free-response astronomy benchmark from Olympiad-style materials (300 questions, including image-linked items) to test whether fine-tuning a language model on a scientific domain still pays off now that general-purpose models have gotten much stronger. Comparing open-weight and API-served general-purpose, multimodal, and astronomy-specialized models, they find that strong general-purpose systems set the highest accuracy baseline, while different evaluation metrics and judges disagree in ways a single leaderboard score would obscure. The result pushes back on assuming domain-specialized AI is always better for science, suggesting the right choice depends on the specific task, deployment setting, and evaluation method used.
