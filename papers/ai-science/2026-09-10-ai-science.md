# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-09-10

## 1. The conservative turn in science: The changing character of knowledge recombination [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Cheng Zhang, Xinkun Liang, Yi Bu, Hongkan Chen
**Date:** September 8, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08468
**Summary:** Using 56 million publications and 816 million citations from OpenAlex (1960–2025), the authors track how the dominant mode of cross-disciplinary knowledge combination has changed, introducing a semantic-distance measure built from paper-embedding classifications of citation types. They find cross-field citations have shifted away from linking entirely disparate knowledge bases toward drawing on partially overlapping fields, alongside more diffuse brokerage positions and heavier reliance on older literature. The pattern points to a science system that is more decentralized but also more conservative in how it recombines ideas, with direct implications for how funding agencies design cross-disciplinary programs.

## 2. AI-Assisted Writing Is Growing Fastest Among Less Established Scientists in Non-English-Speaking Countries [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Jialin Liu, Yongyuan He, Zhihan Zheng, Yi Bu, Chaoqun Ni
**Date:** November 19, 2025 (updated September 6, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.15872
**Summary:** Analyzing over two million full-text biomedical papers on PubMed Central from 2021–2024, the authors use a distribution-based method to estimate how much AI-generated content appears in scientific writing over time. They find AI-assisted writing rose sharply after ChatGPT's release, with the largest increases among researchers in countries with lower English proficiency, those earlier in their careers, and those at lower-ranked or less-cited institutions. The results suggest generative writing tools may be narrowing longstanding linguistic and institutional barriers to publishing in English-dominated science, with modest accompanying gains in productivity among adopters.

## 3. CABAL: Multi-Agent Simulacra for Tracing the Effects of Collusive Bidding in Peer Review [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Jicheng Zhou, Kemou Li, Kahim Wong, Zheyuan Li, Zhuan Shi, Fengpeng Li, Haiwei Wu, Jiantao Zhou
**Date:** September 4, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05227
**Summary:** Motivated by real reports of reviewers coordinating bids during the AAAI-27 cycle, the authors build a multi-agent simulation with LLM-driven reviewer agents to test how collusive bidding rings distort paper assignment and scoring, holding the rest of the conference environment fixed. Their affinity-guided collusion strategy more than doubles a ring's success at capturing target papers and biases assigned reviewers' scores upward by roughly two points, while leaving conference-wide averages only modestly affected. Existing bid-phase collusion detectors catch only a limited share of these attacks, exposing a gap between plausible-looking safeguards and real protection against coordinated manipulation.

## 4. From Citations to Contributions: LLM-Assisted Credit Scoring of Research Articles [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Sana Ebrahimi, Suraj Shetiya, Abolfazl Asudeh
**Date:** September 7, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.07673
**Summary:** The authors propose scoring a paper's scientific credit by separating its own original contribution from the prior work it builds on, rather than treating every citation as an equally weighted signal of influence. Their "contribution tree" framework uses LLMs as noisy comparative estimators of local importance within a paper's structure, then propagates these estimates across citation graphs to produce corpus-level, contribution-based influence scores. Experiments suggest the approach captures genuine contribution signal beyond what citation counts or other surface-level heuristics provide, offering a possible building block for fairer research evaluation.

## 5. An LLM-Associated Register Shift in Korean Journal Abstracts: A Morphology-Aware Excess-Vocabulary Study, 2018-2026 [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Aron Lee
**Date:** September 7, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.07447
**Summary:** Adapting the "excess vocabulary" method used to detect AI-assisted writing in English, the author analyzes nearly 400,000 Korean-language journal abstracts from 2018–2026 for a sudden rise in words associated with LLM-generated text. The data show no shift in 2023, an onset in late 2024, and a rise through 2025 that flattens by mid-2026, with conservative estimates implying a double-digit share of 2026 Korean abstracts carry detectable LLM involvement even after controlling for translation artifacts and subject matter. The findings extend evidence that generative AI is reshaping scientific writing conventions well beyond English-language journals.
