# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-10-22

## 1. Large Teams Consistently Develop Science [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Yiling Lin, Lulin Yang, Lingfei Wu
**Date:** October 20, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27649
**Summary:** The authors defend their earlier finding, made with the disruption index (D), that large research teams tend to build on existing knowledge while small teams more often upend it, against a rival measure (oneD) that claimed the opposite. Using Nobel Prize case studies, mathematical analysis, and large-scale publication data, they show oneD actually misclassifies developmental work as disruptive and suffers from the reference-list-length sensitivity it was designed to fix, whereas D does not. The result reaffirms that team size shapes the kind of scientific contribution produced and clarifies how to measure disruption versus development in research output.

## 2. Are explicit novelty claims in research abstracts associated with higher ChatGPT scores? [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Kayvan Kousha, Mike Thelwall
**Date:** October 18, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26947
**Summary:** Using nearly 100,000 journal articles submitted to the UK's Research Excellence Framework 2021, the authors test whether abstracts that explicitly claim novelty receive higher quality scores from ChatGPT than those that don't. They find such claims are associated with higher scores across all four REF panels, with the gap concentrated in the "originality" dimension rather than significance or rigour, and that novelty-claiming abstracts rank more favorably under ChatGPT than under REF peer-review scores. The findings raise a concrete concern for using LLMs in research assessment: scores may partly reward how researchers phrase their claims rather than the underlying quality of the work.

## 3. AI and The Enclosure of the Scientific Commons [Science Policy]

**Authors:** P. K. G. Williams
**Date:** October 19, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27251
**Summary:** This article applies the economic and political theory of commons and enclosure, drawn from the history of English agricultural common lands, to today's scientific literature, which the author treats as an information commons. It argues that AI supplies tools that make a previously infeasible "enclosure" of that commons possible, converting shared scholarly knowledge into private wealth while degrading the surrounding scholarly ecosystem through AI-generated "slop." The piece offers a conceptual framework, rather than empirical evidence, for understanding why AI's effect on science may resemble historical enclosure rather than a simple productivity boost.

## 4. Opening Knowledge Gaps Drives Scientific Progress [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Kara Kedrick, Wenlong Yang, Thomas Gebhart, Yang Wang, Russell J. Funk
**Date:** October 19, 2026 (v2; first posted September 26, 2025)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21899
**Summary:** The authors develop a computational-topology method to detect "knowledge gaps" — unexplored connections between scientific concepts — and apply it to over 34 million papers spanning 1900–2020. Papers that open such gaps by linking previously unconnected ideas earn fewer citations in their first five years but substantially more over twenty, and are more disruptive and more subject to delayed recognition than papers introducing novel concept pairs without opening a gap. The pattern replicates across five additional databases and patent data, suggesting research funders and evaluators who rely on short-term citation counts may systematically undervalue the most generative work.

## 5. InsightRAG: Aggregating Distributed Scholarly Judgments for Scientific Survey Generation [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Yuquan Lin, Wenshuo Zhang, Yilin Ye, Shuchang Xu, Zhao Chen, Weiqi Wang, Huamin Qu
**Date:** October 20, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28085
**Summary:** The authors build InsightRAG, a retrieval-augmented system for automatic survey generation that mines authors' own Related Work sections — rather than searching the open literature from scratch — to recover how the research community has already organized a topic's citations and concepts. It aggregates these author-written judgments into a taxonomy under a minimum-conflict objective, which then guides and bounds the generated survey's content. Across ten topics the method improves scope coverage, taxonomy quality, and citation reliability relative to prior automatic survey generators, pointing to a way of grounding AI-written literature reviews in human scholarly consensus rather than unconstrained retrieval.
