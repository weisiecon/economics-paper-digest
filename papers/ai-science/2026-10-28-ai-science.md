# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-10-28

## 1. The Hidden Costs of Gatekeeping in Open Science: Quasi-Experimental Evidence from arXiv's Endorsement Policy Tightening [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Wenchang Wang
**Date:** October 25, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33980
**Summary:** Using metadata from three million arXiv papers and co-authorship networks across eight disciplines, the author studies how arXiv's early-2026 tightening of its endorsement policy affected the entry of first-time submitters. The policy reduced new-author entry, but the drop was concentrated in fields with sparse co-authorship networks, while densely connected fields were largely unaffected; a comparison with a parallel biology preprint server suggests researchers shifted where they posted rather than stopping research altogether. The findings show that a scientific community's network structure shapes who can enter open-access publishing, with implications for inequality in who gets to participate in online scientific production.

## 2. Game theory of academic effort and hiring in the age of AI [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Saptarshi Pal, Peter S. Park
**Date:** October 26, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35802
**Summary:** The authors build a game-theoretic model of academic hiring in which AI has made it cheap to produce a long publication record, eroding publication volume's reliability as a signal of genuine understanding. They classify the pure-strategy equilibria of the resulting two-sided game between early-career researchers (who choose between deep study and AI-assisted resume-building) and recruiters (who choose between interview-based understanding checks and publication-count screening), and use learning simulations to see which behaviors populations converge on. The model offers a framework for designing hiring practices that keep rewarding deep understanding even as AI cheapens the traditional signal of it.

## 3. AI-Augmented Research: Free to Write, Costly to Read [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Wolfgang Kuhle
**Date:** October 23, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32355
**Summary:** This paper models a research ecosystem where AI cuts the cost of writing papers faster than it cuts the cost of reading and verifying them, so time-constrained readers (such as referees) increasingly choose to ignore, trust, or verify work based on author reputation rather than content. As writing costs approach zero, the share of papers that actually get verified shrinks toward zero and a paper's apparent impact comes to depend on its author's reputation alone; readers who turn to AI-generated literature summaries to cope are shown to inherit the summarizing AI's own errors once enough of the underlying literature was written with the same AI. The result is a formal account of how cheap AI writing can erode the self-correcting function of peer scrutiny in science.

## 4. Sealed bets on emerging research topics: a protocol for judging early detection, applied to a detector and to eight research funders [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Sébastien Gracia
**Date:** October 25, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34245
**Summary:** The author proposes a pre-registration protocol ("sealed bets") for rigorously judging whether tools that claim to detect emerging research topics actually work, fixing in advance the candidate population, verdict rule, and chance comparison before outcomes are known. Applied retrospectively to one detector on PubMed/MEDLINE, the detector substantially outperforms chance at picking topics that later surge; applied to eight research funders' grant-topic lists, the detector's picks are far more concentrated in future "champion" topics than are the topics those funders actually funded most. The protocol gives funding agencies and policymakers a falsifiable way to evaluate forecasting tools before relying on them to steer research funding toward promising directions.

## 5. Interpreting LLM Scores in Peer Review: Alignment and Divergence Across Prompts, Domains, and Manuscript Features [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Wenqiang Qin, Renlong Jie, Yanjun Qian, Salma Srifi
**Date:** October 26, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35098
**Summary:** Comparing four LLMs against human scores on 7,083 public ICLR 2024 submissions, the authors test how prompt design, review condition, and manuscript features affect how closely an LLM's review score tracks human reviewers' judgments. They find that adding area-chair guidance sharply reduces one model's divergence from human ratings and absolute error (though it barely moves rank correlation), that domain differences in alignment persist even after calibration, and that superficial manuscript features like title length and formula density are systematically associated with scoring divergence. The results argue that any use of LLM scores in peer review needs evidence tailored to the specific scoring procedure and context, since alignment with human judgment is not a fixed property of the model.
