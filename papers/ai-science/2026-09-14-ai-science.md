# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-09-14

## 1. Governing AI Research Through Peer Review: A Mixed-Methods Study of the Longitudinal Effects of Ethics Flags Across Resubmissions [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Kento Nishi, Alec Laprevotte, Isaiah Bullock, Mfoniso Andrew
**Date:** September 9, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10740
**Summary:** The authors track ICLR submissions that received ethics flags and were later resubmitted publicly, coding how 446 resubmissions changed once review ended and the original reviewers no longer oversaw the project, then supplement this with interviews and rebuttal analysis on 25 cases. They find that in 83% of cases authors leave the flagged concern unaddressed or reframe the manuscript without altering the underlying methods, and that authors often concede a concern during rebuttal but drop it after rejection once reviewers are no longer watching. The results suggest ethics review mostly reshapes how work is presented rather than what is studied, leading the authors to recommend disclosing prior ethics flags at resubmission so accountability carries over.

## 2. Mentorship resources and citation-elite journal publication trajectories after training: Evidence from bioscience mentor-mentee networks [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Hao Zeng, Yi Bu, Qingshan Zhou, Hongkan Chen
**Date:** September 11, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12564
**Summary:** Drawing on 239,931 bioscience mentor-mentee pairs from the Academic Family Tree linked to OpenAlex, the authors use matching and count-model methods to test how mentorship-era resources and post-graduation continuity in topics and coauthor networks relate to later publishing in citation-elite journals. Close mentor collaboration and larger peer groups predict more elite publishing during training but less afterward, while retaining the same coauthor network is the most consistently positive predictor of later output, and early topic continuity helps at first but fades as researchers broaden their agendas. The findings imply that converting training-era advantages into a sustained career depends less on the mentorship itself than on how mentees actively maintain or reshape their relational and intellectual ties afterward.

## 3. Wavering Oracles: Selective Updating and Correlated Failures in LLMs and Their Implications for Scientific Workflows [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Xiaoshn Nee, Haobo Zhong, Xiaomin Ni
**Date:** September 10, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11428
**Summary:** Using a controlled benchmark of 17,055 trajectories across ten LLMs, the authors measure "selective updating" — whether a model sticks with correct answers, resists misleading suggestions, and accepts valid corrections — as a proxy for reliability in scientific workflows that chain together repeated queries and multiple models. Performance varies enormously across models, from one that adapts well to suggestions to others that either get destabilized by doubt or blindly follow any suggestion, and errors across models are correlated enough that combining seven models behaves like only about 2.6 independent judges. A simple leave-one-family-out selector nonetheless recovers much of the gap between typical and best-case accuracy, suggesting that measuring and combining model-specific failure patterns is a practical way to make multi-model scientific pipelines more trustworthy.
