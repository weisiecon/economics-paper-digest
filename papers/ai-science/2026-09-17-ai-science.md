# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-09-17

## 1. The AI-Enabled Scientific Frontier [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Gabriel Manso, Emma Fu, Neil Thompson
**Date:** September 14, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16258
**Summary:** The authors assembled 2,507 head-to-head comparisons between AI and other scientific-analysis techniques across 27 disciplines, drawn from papers published between 2000 and early 2025, to test how AI's performance as a general scientific method has evolved. They find AI often beats traditional statistics but at much higher computational cost, and in roughly a quarter of cases is both slower and worse — a share that has held steady for a decade — while AI has been closing the gap with scientific computing methods and has outperformed them on over half of comparisons since 2020. The results argue against AI as a universal scientific method, instead framing it as an increasingly capable but cost-constrained addition to a broader, evolving scientific toolkit.

## 2. Quantifying the impact of clinical-academic collaborations [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Mohamad Zeina, Nick McNally, Karl S. Peggs, Parashkev Nachev
**Date:** September 15, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17051
**Summary:** Using bibliometric and administrative records, the authors build a graphical framework to quantify the value that university partnerships add to hospital research output, applying it to England's NIHR Biomedical Research Centre network with UCLH/UCL as the main case study. They find papers co-authored with the university partner had 1.6 times the field-weighted citation impact and were twice as likely to be cited by a patent as hospital-only papers, and that this partnership benefit was largest precisely where local research infrastructure was sparsest. The approach offers funders and policymakers a reusable, open-data method for measuring returns to academic-clinical collaboration and targeting infrastructure investment.

## 3. Dataset repurposing and disruptive AI research [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Yulin Yu, Yong-Yeol Ahn, Daniel M. Romero
**Date:** September 15, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16736
**Summary:** Analyzing more than 10,000 machine learning papers, the authors study how repurposing existing datasets for new uses relates to scientific impact and disruption in AI research. They find repurposed datasets rarely gain much visibility in the short run, but papers that repurpose data — and especially later papers that build on someone else's repurposed data — tend to be more disruptive and more cited, and that repurposing is disproportionately done by larger, more prestigious teams with academia-industry ties. The findings position data repurposing as an underappreciated engine of scientific discovery, even though team pedigree alone cannot predict which repurposed datasets will catch on.

## 4. Benchmarking locally hosted language models for journal editorial work on a compact desktop workstation [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Haruka Ozaki
**Date:** September 15, 2026 (v2; originally posted August 31, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11972
**Summary:** Motivated by journals that cannot send unpublished manuscripts to external AI services, the author benchmarks twenty open-weight language models running on a single desktop workstation against eight editorial tasks drawn from a journal's author guidelines and seeded manuscript defects. The best model caught 36 of 40 seeded violations while needing 81GB of memory, but model size did not reliably predict performance, and a simple rule-based checker alone caught 31 violations in a fraction of a second — with the checker and best model together catching all 40. The results suggest small, locally hosted models paired with deterministic checks can meaningfully support editorial screening without relying on external AI vendors.

## 5. Geospatial Metadata Improves Discoverability by Connecting Datasets Across Scientific Disciplines [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Daniel Ebanks, Devika Jain
**Date:** September 15, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16498
**Summary:** Using Harvard Dataverse, the world's largest general-purpose research data repository, the authors show that datasets with incomplete metadata receive fewer citations and fewer resolvable links to other datasets, and that geospatial metadata in particular connects datasets across scientific disciplines about twice as effectively as keyword-based metadata. They build a metadata knowledge graph and fine-tune a small language model to enrich geospatial metadata, raising the share of cross-disciplinary dataset connections from 58.5% to 63.2%. The work points to an AI-assisted route for improving research data infrastructure and, by extension, the reuse and productivity of shared scientific data.
