# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-11-20

## 1. Same Papers, Different Verdicts: Tracing Version Drift in LLM Peer Review [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Jizhou Guo
**Date:** November 16, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20608
**Summary:** The author audited twenty frontier LLMs from OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic as reviewers of ICLR 2027 submissions, using pre-registered tests comparing successive model versions on the same papers. Newer versions of Claude Opus and Gemini accepted far fewer of the same submissions than their predecessors (shifts of 43-79 percentage points), driven by changes in the generated critiques themselves rather than by scoring instructions, and model self-reported percentiles barely tracked human reviewer agreement. The findings show that LLM-based review scores are not stable measurements of paper quality but artifacts of which model version is used, so any threshold built on them needs separate validation against human decisions for each new release.

## 2. Twice-Told Critiques: Claim-Level Homogenization of Peer Review in the LLM Era [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Ilya Gusev
**Date:** November 19, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26286
**Summary:** Using an LLM-text detector alongside a claim-overlap metric, the author measures how much independent reviews of the same paper repeat each other's specific criticisms at ICLR, NeurIPS, ICML, and several journals. Overlap between reviews has risen year over year, and pairs of reviews flagged as LLM-assisted overlap noticeably more than pairs of unflagged reviews, with the gap widening from 2024 to 2027. The results suggest that as reviewers lean on language models, the diversity of independent critique that peer review is supposed to provide is eroding.

## 3. Do Network Mechanisms in Science Vary Across Research Topics? Contextual Relational Hyperevent Models for Large-Scale Bibliometric Data [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Jürgen Lerner, Mark Wittek, Raphael Heiberger
**Date:** November 19, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26052
**Summary:** The authors develop a new statistical model, contextual relational hyperevent models, that can estimate how collaboration and citation patterns such as preferential attachment, gender homophily, and self-citation vary across hundreds of thousands of publications sorted into research topics, something earlier network models could not handle at this scale. Applied to roughly 590,000 oncology papers split into 32 topics, they find these mechanisms differ substantially by topic, with older and more male-dominated subfields showing stronger self-citation and homophily. The work cautions against treating effects estimated on pooled scientometric data as representative of science in general, since the structure of credit and collaboration varies by research area.

## 4. AI Behavioral Science: A Framework and Agenda [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Matthew O. Jackson, Qiaozhu Mei, Stephanie W. Wang, Yutong Xie, Walter Yuan, Seth Benzell, Erik Brynjolfsson, Colin F. Camerer, James Evans, Brian Jabarian, Jon Kleinberg, Juanjuan Meng, Sendhil Mullainathan, Asuman Ozdaglar, Thomas Pfeiffer, Moshe Tennenholtz, Robb Willer, Diyi Yang, Teng Ye
**Date:** August 17, 2025 (v4, November 17, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.13323
**Summary:** A large interdisciplinary group of economists and computer scientists lay out a research agenda for "AI Behavioral Science," organized around three tasks: borrowing social-science methods to measure AI systems' own biases and behavioral tendencies, using AI as a new tool for studying human behavior at larger scale and lower cost, and modeling the feedback loops that emerge as humans and AI increasingly interact. They argue each strand is already underway but underdeveloped relative to its importance, and sketch open questions linking AI evaluation, computational social science, and the economic and political consequences of human-AI interaction. The paper functions as an agenda-setting piece meant to pull these separate literatures into a shared framework.

## 5. Coordinated Use of Large Language Models in Academic Research [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Vladislav Marinov
**Date:** November 19, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25502
**Summary:** The author proposes a methodological framework for using multiple large language models together in research, arguing that simply adding more models does not improve reliability unless their roles are deliberately organized. The framework rests on principles such as keeping models procedurally independent, assigning them distinct functions, treating disagreement and agreement between models as diagnostic signals, and routing uncertain conclusions through external verification, illustrated with cases drawn from the author's own research practice. It frames the shift from single-model to coordinated multi-model use as a change in how research is organized procedurally, with the human researcher retaining final judgment and responsibility.
