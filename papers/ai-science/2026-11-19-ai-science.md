# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-11-19

## 1. Large Language Models Have Not Yet Transformed Science [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Ron Bekkerman, Nichole Li, Bahareh Kargar, Mark Somers
**Date:** November 17, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22975
**Summary:** The authors analyze 2.5 million full-text papers across five disciplines from 2015 to 2025 to see whether the arrival of LLMs in late 2022 broke pre-existing trends in how science is written and practiced. Across lexical, structural, epistemological, and social dimensions, most trends that were already underway before 2023 simply continued, with only a few exceptions, such as faster growth of large computer-science coauthor teams in Asia and a seeming drop in biomedical topic diversity that actually reflects standardized article formats rather than narrower research. The study argues LLM adoption has not yet produced a measurable break in scientific practice and offers a baseline for tracking whether that changes.

## 2. Embedding or Bridging? AI4Science Teams Differ More in Knowledge Recombination Than in Problem Novelty [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Yun Fu, Jielan Ding
**Date:** November 17, 2026 (v2, November 18, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22366
**Summary:** Using 489,409 AI4Science articles published between 1990 and 2025, the authors classify research teams by how AI specialists and domain scientists are organizationally arranged, from fully embedded units to various forms of cross-unit bridging. Teams organized around cross-unit bridging, especially AI-oriented bridging, draw on substantially broader combinations of prior knowledge than embedded teams, but this breadth barely affects how novel the research problems themselves are, and does not reliably translate into higher citation impact or more Elite-4 journal placements. The findings suggest that how AI and domain expertise are organizationally combined shapes what kind of scientific contribution emerges, rather than any one structure being uniformly best.

## 3. Theses by Publication in Spain: Output, Authorship and Integrity Concerns [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Álvaro Cabezas-Clavijo, Nazaret Barriga, Rafael Repiso
**Date:** November 18, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24350
**Summary:** The authors analyze 338 "thesis by publication" compendia defended in Spain in 2025-2026, examining the journal articles behind them and the doctoral candidates' authorship roles. Most candidates were first authors on high-quartile journal articles, but the study also documents widespread reliance on so-called grey publishers like MDPI and Frontiers, along with scattered questionable practices such as delisted journals, self-edited special issues, and articles reused across multiple theses. The authors call for clearer national rules on authorship and venue quality for this increasingly common doctoral format.

## 4. Where Do Highly Cited Scientists Stand? A Multi-Dimensional Analysis of Positional Distribution in the Top 2% Ranking [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Yuehai Li, Zuguang Gu
**Date:** November 17, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22817
**Summary:** The authors introduce "positional enrichment," a method that compares how concentrated a country's, institution's, or subfield's scientists are near the top versus the bottom of the Top 2% Scientists list, rather than simply counting how many scientists make the list. They find that subfields have globally stable head-, middle-, or tail-enriched profiles, while countries and institutions vary by which subfields they are strong in, so a country's prominence mostly reflects which fields it specializes in rather than outperforming peers within the same field. The results reframe the widely used ranking as a subfield-stratified structure rather than a flat measure of individual scientific influence, with implications for how such rankings are used in institutional evaluation.
