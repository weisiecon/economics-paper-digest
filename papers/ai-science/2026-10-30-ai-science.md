# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-10-30

## 1. What if automating AI R&D triggers an intelligence explosion? [Science Policy]

**Authors:** Alan Chan, Christoph Winter, Andrew Barto, Jakub Pachocki, Geoffrey Hinton, Eric Horvitz, Yoshua Bengio, Dawn Song, Jack Clark, Hilary Greaves, Anton Korinek, Samuel Hammond, Thore Graepel, Ben Bariach, Philip H. S. Torr, Sheila A. McIlraith, Jeff Clune, Sam Manning, Girish Sastry, Tom Davidson, Daniel Eth, Sören Mindermann
**Date:** October 26, 2026 (v2; originally posted September 28, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.36054
**Summary:** A large group of AI researchers and policy scholars argue that as AI systems increasingly automate the work of AI research and development itself, progress could compound into an "intelligence explosion" that compresses years of advances into months. They review preliminary evidence that this dynamic is already underway and lay out the risks if capability growth outpaces society's ability to adapt, including loss of control over superhuman systems and erosion of institutional checks on power. The paper calls on policymakers to gain more visibility into AI R&D automation now and to develop tools for steering or constraining a potential intelligence explosion before it happens.

## 2. GenAI-Assisted Writing and Academic Performance: Evidence from the Social and Behavioral Sciences [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Dragan Filimonovic, Christian Rutzer, Conny Wunsch
**Date:** October 23, 2026 (v2; originally posted October 2, 2025)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.02408
**Summary:** Using lexical markers that surged after ChatGPT's late-2022 release, the authors classify social-science authors as GenAI-assisted writers and compare their publication records to matched peers in a difference-in-differences design. GenAI-assisted writers saw publication output rise roughly 72% relative to similar non-adopting authors in 2025, driven mostly by more projects reaching publication rather than placement in better journals, with the largest gains among early-career researchers and authors writing far from English. The results suggest generative AI is lowering writing-related barriers to publication, though the benefits concentrate on output quantity rather than quality as measured by journal rank.

## 3. AI-Augmented Research: Free to Write, Costly to Read [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Wolfgang Kuhle
**Date:** October 23, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32355
**Summary:** This theoretical paper models a world where AI makes writing papers cheap while reading and verifying them remains costly, so time-constrained referees must choose between ignoring, verifying, or simply trusting submissions based on author reputation. As writing costs fall toward zero, the model shows the pool of actually-verified papers shrinks: unknown authors stop getting read at all, and even established authors escape scrutiny, so a paper's perceived credibility rests increasingly on reputation rather than content. The paper also shows that AI-generated literature summaries, which readers use to cope with the flood of output, become unreliable once enough of the literature is written by the same AI, because the summaries then inherit and compound that AI's own errors.

## 4. The Hidden Costs of Gatekeeping in Open Science: Quasi-Experimental Evidence from arXiv's Endorsement Policy Tightening [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Wenchang Wang
**Date:** October 25, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33980
**Summary:** Exploiting arXiv's early-2026 tightening of its endorsement requirement as a natural experiment, the author compares first-time submitters before and after the change across eight disciplines using co-authorship network data from three million papers. The policy reduced entry of new authors, but the effect was concentrated in fields with sparse co-authorship networks, while densely connected fields were largely unaffected; a comparison with a biology preprint server suggests researchers shifted where they post rather than stopping research altogether. The findings show that a scientific community's network structure shapes who can clear "vouching" gatekeeping mechanisms, adding a new angle on how open-science infrastructure can unintentionally entrench inequality in who gets to participate.

## 5. More of the same? Are scientific papers losing originality? [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Ilan Doron-Arad, Elchanan Mossel
**Date:** October 27, 2026 (v2; originally posted September 26, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32963
**Summary:** Using semantic language models to screen over 26,000 full-text papers across eight fields from 2015–2025, the authors measure how textually and conceptually similar each paper is to a fixed sample of earlier work, controlling for the mechanical effect of a growing literature. They find resemblance to prior work was flat before 2021 but then more than tripled by 2025, driven not by a few authors recycling more but by the practice spreading broadly across the literature, with almost none of the increase explained by citations. The pattern held up across robustness checks and replicated in an independent database, suggesting that — at least along this measurable dimension — science is becoming less original just as AI writing tools have become widely available.
