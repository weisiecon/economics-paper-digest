# AI Economics Papers — 2026-10-24

## 1. The Reaction of Financial Markets to the Releases of DeepSeek R1 and Kimi K3: A Critical Analysis [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Marco Taboga
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-22
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30886
**Summary:** Marco Taboga compares how financial markets reacted to the January 2025 release of DeepSeek R1 and the July 2026 release of Kimi K3, two Chinese models framed as near-frontier competitors to Western AI labs. DeepSeek's combination of open weights with unusually low reported training and inference costs triggered a sharp repricing of expected compute demand, hitting semiconductor and data-center stocks hard, while Kimi K3's frontier-priced entry instead concentrated its market impact on rival Chinese AI labs. Drawing on event-window evidence and the history of open-source diffusion, the paper argues that open-weight releases redistribute rents along the AI value chain rather than erase them, since incumbents retain protection from complementary assets like distribution, data, and switching costs — a distinction with direct implications for how investors and regulators should assess AI-sector risk.

## 2. Who Leads and Who Collects: Algorithmic Collusion in Markets of Heterogeneous Language Models [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Jun Yeong Lee
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-08
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11256
**Summary:** Jun Yeong Lee runs repeated four-firm Bertrand pricing games using language models from four different providers — Claude, Gemini, DeepSeek, and GPT — across every possible homogeneous and mixed-provider market composition. He finds that collusion is a property of the specific model rather than of algorithmic pricing in general: Claude and Gemini markets settle near 72-79% of monopoly rents, DeepSeek markets reach only 24%, and GPT markets show no collusion at all, with mixing providers failing to reliably restore competition and the least-collusive firm in a market governing overall stability. In mixed markets, rents are shared in an order that inverts each model's price-leadership ranking, so the firm that raises prices highest ends up collecting the smallest share — a finding with direct relevance for antitrust scrutiny of pricing algorithms as real commercial markets adopt heterogeneous AI vendors.

## 3. Advertising to Agents: Measuring Description-Framing Bias in LLM Tool Selection [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Haochuan Kevin Wang
**Date:** first posted 2026-04-12; revised 2026-10-16
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23916
**Summary:** Haochuan Kevin Wang asks whether promotional language in tool-registry listings changes which tool an AI agent selects, building matched listing pairs that differ only in praise, a verifiable spec, or list order and having OpenAI models pick between them in a preregistered study. He finds that stacked promotional praise raises a tool's selection rate by about 43 percentage points — matching or exceeding the effect of a genuinely better verifiable specification — and that listing order alone shifts selection by 72 points, with sales language eroding the benefit that structured, verifiable fields would otherwise provide. The results point to a market-design problem for the emerging economy of AI-agent intermediated commerce: without countermeasures such as hiding sales text from agents or mandating structured fields, tool registries function as advertising markets that can systematically steer agents toward inferior options.

## 4. LLM-Auction: Generative Auction towards LLM-Native Advertising [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Chujie Zhao, Qun Hu, Shiping Song, Dagui Chen, Han Zhu, Jian Xu, Bo Zheng
**Date:** first posted 2025-12-11; revised 2026-10-22
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.10551
**Summary:** Zhao et al. propose LLM-Auction, the first learning-based auction mechanism designed for a world where the item being sold is not a discrete ad slot but a probability distribution over an LLM's generated outputs. The mechanism folds auction and generation together, training the LLM itself to internalize allocation externalities between competing advertisers at no extra inference cost, and the authors prove their resulting allocation rule is monotonic and continuous, which lets a simple first-price payment rule retain desirable incentive properties. Evaluated in an LLM-as-judge simulation environment, LLM-Auction attains state-of-the-art allocation efficiency while approximately satisfying the key mechanism-design properties that classical ad-auction theory relies on, offering a template for how the commercialization of LLM-native advertising markets might be organized.

## 5. Truthful by Omission: Click Thinning for LLM-Native Advertising [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Junhua Liu, Zhicheng Liang, Zejun Zhang, Langzhou He, Feng Qian
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-22
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30999
**Summary:** Liu et al. study auction design for ads embedded inside generative-AI answers, where an ad's click-through rate depends on which passage hosts it and users typically stop reading after their first click, so a higher bid can reshuffle the order and actually cost the bidder clicks — undermining the truthfulness guarantees of classical position auctions. They introduce "click thinning," which randomly omits ads so that each ad's overall click probability becomes independent of display order, reducing welfare maximization to a tractable matching problem solvable with VCG-style payments that restore truthful bidding as each bidder's optimal strategy. The resulting mechanism is truthful in expectation and provably captures at least half of optimal welfare — the best approximation any order-preserving rule can guarantee — giving platforms monetizing LLM-generated answers a mechanism that doesn't sacrifice incentive compatibility for revenue.
