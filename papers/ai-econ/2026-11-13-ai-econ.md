# AI Economics Papers — 2026-11-13

## 1. Accuracy Is Not Welfare: Evaluating AI Buyers under Strategic Price Complexity in a Synthetic Duopoly [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Ning Jiaqu
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-12
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16471
**Summary:** This paper builds a synthetic duopoly in which AI shopping agents compare prices on a fixed menu and in empirical correlated-equilibrium games, contrasting a calculator-style pipeline against one that relies on verified-total disclosure. It finds that the calculator pipeline selects the accuracy-optimal offer far more often (84% vs. 68.57%), yet disclosure delivers a 20-unit higher consumer-worst-case surplus, and that re-solving equilibria under scenario resampling can erase that gap entirely. The result shows that accuracy, loss severity, seller incentives and welfare incidence are distinct quantities that must be measured separately as AI buying agents become common, with direct implications for how sellers will adapt pricing strategy to AI-mediated shopping.

## 2. Data-center growth impacts household electricity costs more through wholesale prices than through grid reinforcement [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Lingdong Zhou, Wenjia Xia, Jiyu Wang, Fei Ding, Xiangqi Zhu
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-11
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16095
**Summary:** Using 371 announced Texas data-center projects totaling 38.9 GW of AI-driven demand by 2030, the authors link power-system expansion, wholesale electricity markets, transmission reinforcement and retail cost allocation to quantify who bears the cost of AI compute buildout. They find residential electricity costs rise by 1.64 cents/kWh in a representative case, with 68% of that increase coming from higher wholesale prices rather than the 32% from regulated transmission charges, and show the split is highly sensitive to generation siting and cost-allocation rules (0.92 to 1.87 cents/kWh across policy scenarios). The findings matter for AI policy because they show the aggregate welfare cost of the AI compute race is being transmitted to ordinary ratepayers mainly through market price effects, not through the infrastructure investment itself, so cost-allocation rules are the more powerful lever for protecting households.
