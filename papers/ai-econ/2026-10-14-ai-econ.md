# AI Economics Papers — 2026-10-14

## 1. Is the business of frontier AI companies selling tokens? Value capture and equity prices [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Andrew J. Peterson
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-09
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14145
**Summary:** The author assembles reverse cash-flow calculations for OpenAI and Anthropic to ask whether selling token access alone can support their valuations, finding combined year-ten revenue requirements of $2.79 trillion or $733 billion depending on the financial assumptions used. Public filings and partnership announcements point to additional income streams beyond model access, such as equity stakes and payments tied to pharmaceuticals, robotics, and materials-science ventures. The analysis situates these arrangements within the theory of firm boundaries, arguing that frontier AI companies' viability may depend on capturing value from downstream industries rather than from selling intelligence as a standalone commodity service.

## 2. Chaining Tasks, Redefining Work: A Theory of AI Automation [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Mert Demirer, John J. Horton, Nicole Immorlica, Brendan Lucier, Peyman Shahidi
**Date:** first posted 2026-06-14; revised 2026-10-02
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.15960
**Summary:** The authors model production as a sequence of interdependent Leontief steps that a firm can assign to human labor, AI-augmented labor, or contiguous automated "chains," and solve for the firm's cost-minimizing assignment. They show that chaining can overturn standard comparative-advantage logic — steps where humans hold an advantage are sometimes optimally handed to AI as part of a chain — and that returns to improving AI quality can be non-monotone, producing a pattern resembling a productivity J-curve. Matching the model against observed automation patterns, they find that AI-exposed steps empirically cluster together within workflows, giving firms and policymakers a structural account of where automation gains concentrate.

## 3. Auditing AI-Washing in German Startups: A Mixed-Methods Study of Marketing Claims and Perceptions [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** A Pranav, Luke Hartmann, Anne Lauscher
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-08
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11788
**Summary:** The authors audit 100 German AI startups' marketing claims against a seven-dimension codebook and pair this with 63 expert interviews, finding capability exaggeration is widespread: claims lack inspectable evidence, performance figures omit methodology, and limitations go unacknowledged. Repeated exposure to such exaggerated claims measurably eroded consumer trust in subsequent AI marketing, while 38% of audited startups were flagged for moderate or serious ethical concerns in domains such as surveillance and automated decision-making. The findings document an information asymmetry in the market for AI products — founders over-claim capability to attract investors who cannot verify it — and the authors argue this calls for disclosure requirements enforced by regulators and investors.
