# AI Economics Papers — 2026-10-30

## 1. AI Exposure and Adoption among U.S. Firms [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Matthew Dey, Emin Dinlersoz, Lucia Foster, Cheryl Grim, John Haltiwanger, Peter B. Meyer, Sabrina Wulff Pabilonia, Aditya Pande, Martha Stinson, Sean Wang, Zoltan Wolf
**Date:** first posted September 2026
**Source:** https://www2.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/ces/CES-WP-26-61.pdf (Census CES Working Paper 26-61; also posted as BLS Working Paper No. 591)
**Summary:** The authors link 14 widely used occupation-based AI exposure measures, built from BLS occupational employment data, to direct firm-level AI adoption reports from the Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey. They find exposure is positively and statistically significantly associated with realized adoption, but a one-standard-deviation increase in exposure raises adoption probability by only 4-11 percentage points (falling to 1-8 points with year and sub-sector fixed effects), and the exposure-adoption relationship varies considerably across measures, firm-size classes, and sectors. The results imply that exposure indices — the standard proxy used throughout the literature to study AI's economic effects — are informative but incomplete signals of actual adoption, with direct implications for how that literature's estimates should be interpreted.

## 2. It Matters Who Gets Automated: Monetary Policy in an AI Economy [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Alina Ermolaeva, Hannah Seidl
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-12
**Source:** https://www.diw.de/documents/publikationen/73/diw_01.c.1022699.de/dp2184.pdf (DIW Berlin Discussion Paper No. 2184)
**Summary:** Ermolaeva and Seidl build a two-sector heterogeneous-agent New Keynesian model with task-based production, in which AI automates part of the work in one sector while leaving a second sector shielded, and use it to ask whether AI changes monetary policy transmission. They find that in the AI steady state the economy holds more capital and labor income shifts from exposed workers (who lose hours) to shielded workers (who gain through higher wages), and that an identical interest-rate cut stimulates macroeconomic aggregates more in the AI economy because the deeper capital stock delivers a larger initial investment response on a permanently larger base. The upshot is that monetary policy amplification in an AI economy runs specifically through the workers AI does not replace, making the sectoral incidence of automation a first-order determinant of policy transmission.

## 3. Transformative AI and Demand Bottlenecks [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Phurichai Rungcharoenkitkul
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-12
**Source:** https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-1381-transformative-ai-and-demand-bottlenecks (BIS Working Paper No. 1381)
**Summary:** Rungcharoenkitkul builds an endogenous growth model in which innovation is financed by consumer demand, then asks whether large-scale automation could choke off the very demand that funds continued AI research by raising investment's share of output at the expense of the consumer market. Calibrating the model to available evidence, he finds a "demand bottleneck" scenario — in which AI progress stalls for lack of demand-financed returns to innovation — highly plausible, and the model projects more moderate long-run growth than several recent transformative-AI studies. The paper also draws out policy implications, including for the natural rate of interest, making the demand side of the economy a central constraint on how much growth AI can ultimately deliver.
