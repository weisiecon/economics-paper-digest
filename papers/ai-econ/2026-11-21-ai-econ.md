# AI Economics Papers — 2026-11-21

## 1. AI and the Two Margins of Work: Why Job Losses Show Up Before Job Creation [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Mark Esposito, Liu Zhang
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-12
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17530
**Summary:** The authors build a model in which AI lowers the cost of existing production and also makes new projects worth attempting, and show that because payroll statistics record contraction at incumbents promptly but new-activity creation only late, the same technology can look purely displacing in the short run even when it is not displacing in the long run. Using historical computing-era entry-cohort data and establishment job flows through early 2026, they find incumbents in exposed industries contracted before entrants grew, and that current job flows show no gradient on language-model exposure, consistent with the model's prediction under the observed adoption path. They propose a "net reinstatement ratio" to correct for this reporting lag and argue that existing statistics systematically overstate AI-driven displacement for years after it occurs.

## 2. The opposing informational effects of AI trading [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Hajime Shimao
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-13
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18872
**Summary:** This paper models a competitive market with resource-constrained forecasting in which more capable AI can substitute analysis of public data for private observations, which improves individual forecasts but can reduce the private information that trading aggregates into prices. Using equilibrium calculations, neural-market experiments, and language-model tasks, the author shows wider AI adoption raises the predictive value of prices for unsophisticated observers while lowering it for sophisticated ones, and cuts the benefit of further computation by 44% in the experiments. The results imply that better AI trading tools do not necessarily make markets more informationally efficient, with consequences for who benefits from price information and how much private investment in forecasting is sustained.

## 3. Does the AI Reviewer Alleviate or Aggravate the Maintenance Burden? Evidence from GitHub Copilot Reviewer [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Feiyang Xu, Poonacha K. Medappa, Murat M. Tunc
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-17
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22793
**Summary:** Using the 2025 introduction of GitHub Copilot Reviewer as a natural experiment, the authors study how an AI code reviewer changes maintainers' workload and contributor behavior in open-source projects. They find pull-request submissions and review workload jump after adoption, human contributions decline over time while bot-generated submissions keep rising, and survival analysis shows the AI reviewer speeds up review of human-submitted code but slows review of bot-submitted code. The findings show that an AI tool can simultaneously raise review efficiency and worsen the aggregate maintenance burden on a software platform, by inducing more total contributions than it helps process.

## 4. The Rebundling of Freelance Work in the AI Age [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Caroline Chin, Shagun Kala, Nadav Kunievsky, Teng Liu, Alex Martin Richmond, Carl Shan
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-10
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14708
**Summary:** Comparing the activities people request from ChatGPT with the tasks clients post on the freelance platform Upwork, the authors find that task types more common in ChatGPT usage lose marketplace share on Upwork while less-common ones gain share. They also find AI-related job postings increasingly combine tasks that were rarely bundled together before generative AI, carry substantially larger budgets and more work hours, with only small differences in hourly rates. The results suggest AI is reshaping freelance labor markets less by eliminating demand for tasks AI can do and more by changing which tasks clients outsource together and at what scale.

## 5. Moral AI and the Erosion of Its Own Demand [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Wallice Ao, Jiabin Wu
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-18
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25213
**Summary:** This theoretical paper models an evolutionary setting where individuals choose between a "moral" AI that commits them to cooperation and an unconstrained AI that preserves discretion, and shows that moral AI can sustain cooperation even when cooperative types are initially too scarce to do so unaided. That success is self-undermining: by converting dormant cooperative preferences into committed behavior, moral AI exposes committed users to exploitation, driving down the population share of conditional cooperators under evolutionary selection until equilibrium adoption of moral AI collapses to zero. The result identifies a structural limit on the market for commitment-enabling AI products: their own success in changing behavior can erode the preference base that sustains demand for them.
