# AI Economics Papers — 2026-10-18

## 1. The Macroeconomic Effect of AI: Sizing the Software Engineering Channel [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Alex Blumenfeld, Jonathon Hazell, Chen Lian, Andreas Schaab
**Date:** first posted 2026-09-28
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35793 (NBER Working Paper 35793)
**Summary:** The authors build a market-based, real-time measure of AI's economic impact by tracking how each firm's stock return responds to a broad AI index, conditional on the share of its payroll spent on software engineers, and feed the estimated productivity effect through a macro model. From November 2022 to December 2025, they find AI raised the market's expected present value of software-engineering productivity by the equivalent of a permanent 32.6% gain, implying a 3.6% boost to GDP in their baseline (6.5% if the gain also spills into R&D productivity), with the estimated effect more than doubling by mid-2026 as coding agents improved rapidly. The approach gives policymakers a fast-moving, market-implied gauge of how much of AI's promised macroeconomic payoff is already being priced in through this one channel.

## 2. Do Markets Dream of Artificial Intelligence? Macroeconomic Effects and International Spillovers of AI News Shocks [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Maria Sole Pagliari
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-01
**Source:** https://www.dnb.nl/en/publications/research-publications/working-paper-2026/871-do-markets-dream-of-artificial-intelligence-macroeconomic-effects-and-international-spillovers-of-ai-news-shocks/ (DNB Working Paper 871)
**Summary:** Using a daily factor built from the gap in stock returns between high- and low-AI-exposure firms, the author identifies shocks to market expectations about AI's economic value and traces their effects through a Bayesian VAR for the US and local projections for the euro area. In the US, capital-goods orders rise quickly while broader output and prices adjust only gradually; in the euro area, AI optimism lifts equities, weakens the euro, and pushes up import and energy costs before any productivity payoff materializes. The paper's central finding for policy is that favorable financial and price spillovers from AI news arrive well before the real productivity gains do, creating a timing mismatch that complicates the response of monetary policy.

## 3. Circular relationships among AI firms [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Jon Frost, Rudraksh Kansal, Kumar Rishabh, Vatsala Shreeti, Leanne Si Ying Zhang
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-01
**Source:** https://www.bis.org/publications/bulletin-137-circular-relationships-among-ai-firms (BIS Bulletin No. 137)
**Summary:** The authors assemble a dataset of investment deals among 1,246 AI firms across the compute-to-application supply chain between 2021 and 2025 to measure how much AI investment stays within the AI sector itself. They find that AI-firm targets account for 28.7% of AI firms' outbound investment by value and that 55.2% of capital flowing into AI firms comes from other AI firms, with close to half of that AI-to-AI investment value also tied to a commercial supply relationship between the same two companies. They argue these circular ties partly reflect genuine economic motives, such as locking in scarce inputs under information asymmetries, but that the resulting web of cross-holdings also concentrates macroeconomic risk and makes the sector's true financial exposure harder for outsiders to see.

## 4. Liability and Pricing of Dual-Use AI [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Joshua S. Gans
**Date:** first posted 2026-09-28
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35828 (NBER Working Paper 35828)
**Summary:** Gans models an AI provider's optimal liability exposure when its product can enable both attack and defense, showing that raising the common price charged to all users can substitute for liability by discouraging low-value defensive use without changing attackers' behavior, while compensating victims instead weakens defense and raises attackers' profits. The paper derives how the optimal liability rule trades these effects off against the risk of pricing out legitimate users, finding that stronger competition among AI providers generally lowers optimal liability, that a monopolist can justify partial but not full liability, and that when usage-limiting guardrails are available, competitive markets favor making them universal rather than relying on liability at all. The result is a framework for setting AI liability rules that depends explicitly on market structure, giving regulators a basis for why the right liability regime for a dominant AI provider should differ from the right regime under competition.

## 5. Decentralized LLM Workflows via Sequential Auctions [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Ian Gemp, Shubhendu Trivedi, Adam Fisch, Fantine Huot, Michael Kaisers, Mirella Lapata, William W. Cohen, Alekh Agarwal, Kate Larson, Jacob Eisenstein
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-15
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22201
**Summary:** The authors model a market in which self-interested AI agents complete multi-step tasks by bidding sequentially for the right to work on each step, extending prior theory that relied on an unrealistic assumption of perfectly identical competing agents. They show that pure, unconstrained competition between identical agents drives profit margins to zero and collapses the market, just as a complete absence of competition also causes market failure, so a viable agent marketplace must sit in between, with agents learning to shade their bids to protect positive margins while keeping the auction mechanism efficient enough to still complete tasks. Comparing four bidding algorithms across three standard auction formats, they find this "imperfect competition" middle ground reliably sustains both profitable participation and high task-completion rates, offering an early blueprint for how decentralized markets of AI agents could remain economically viable.
