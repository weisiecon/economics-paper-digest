# AI Economics Papers — 2026-10-26

## 1. Monetizing Intelligence: Advertising and Subscription Design on LLM Platforms [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Yuhang Ji, Fasheng Xu, Tianjun Feng
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-23
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32945
**Summary:** The authors model a monopolistic LLM platform that jointly sets response quality, advertising intensity, and subscription prices across ad-supported, partly-ad-supported, and ad-free tiers, where heavier advertising lowers user value but higher quality raises what advertisers will pay for exposure. They show advertising can fund meaningful service for users who would get none under pure subscription, but degrading the middle tier also makes the ad-free alternative look relatively better, letting the platform raise ad-free prices even though that tier's own quality hasn't changed — producing "utility polarization" where broader access and some users' losses coexist. Counterintuitively, cheaper compute, while raising quality and cutting ad intensity per interaction, can still raise the platform's total ad revenue because better service makes the remaining ad-exposed interactions more valuable, making tiered menu design, not AI's per-unit cost alone, central to how falling compute costs reach user welfare.

## 2. When AI Gets Cheaper, Do Prices Follow? Information Delays and Algorithmic Pricing [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Krish Tadigotla
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-20
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31693
**Summary:** Tadigotla uses a simulated duopoly market with Q-learning pricing agents to ask whether falling AI operating costs actually reach consumers as lower prices, and whether the speed at which firms observe rivals' prices and can revise their own changes the answer. Across nearly a thousand training runs spanning 16 conditions that vary price-information delay, repricing frequency, cost shocks, and firm asymmetry, he finds that even with the fastest information and most frequent repricing, firms pass through only 44-56% of a cost cut to consumers, and the learned pricing policies leave consumer surplus below the static Nash benchmark in every condition tested. The delay-repricing interaction itself is statistically indistinguishable from zero, so the shortfall traces to the pricing algorithms' learned behavior rather than the specific frictions varied — a caution against assuming cheaper AI mechanically becomes cheaper AI services, though the author stops short of calling it collusion.
