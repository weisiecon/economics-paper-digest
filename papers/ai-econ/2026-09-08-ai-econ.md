# AI Economics Papers — 2026-09-08

## 1. From Prompt to Purchase: How AI Brand Recommendations Move Consumers on the Open Web [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Michael Iannelli, Alan Ai
**Date:** first posted 2026-06-09; revised 2026-08-31
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.10907
**Summary:** The authors join an opt-in clickstream panel to the same users' ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini conversation logs and use a matched backward-placebo design to isolate the causal effect of an unprompted chatbot brand mention from the confound of existing customers' own behavior. They find that a mention of a brand with no recent user engagement raises that brand's own-name search by 4.3 percentage points, direct site visits by 2.4 points, and retailer-page visits by 1.0 point relative to matched placebos. The result is the first credible market-level measurement of conversational AI as a consumer-acquisition channel invisible to standard web analytics, with direct implications for advertising economics and platform competition.

## 2. Does Rank Still Matter? Position Bias When AI Agents Shop on Our Behalf [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Davood Wadi, Yu Ma
**Date:** first posted 2026-08-24; revised 2026-08-28
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22697
**Summary:** Randomizing the order of 100 hotel listings across 5,000 sessions with four large language models acting as shopping agents, the authors compare AI search and purchase behavior against human field data on the same choice task. They find AI agents inspect far more listings than humans and never decline to buy, that listing position still predicts inspection but only weakly and non-monotonically (the middle of the page is inspected least, not the bottom), and that despite model-specific quirks all agents converge on the same undominated option. As AI agents take over more consumer search, the results suggest classic ranking-based advantages for sellers erode in favor of genuine product attributes, with implications for platform design and retail competition.

## 3. Incumbent Advantage: Brand Bias and Cognitive Manipulation Dynamics in LLM Recommendation Systems [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Xi Chu, Yupeng Hou
**Date:** first posted 2026-06-16; revised 2026-08-21
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.17443
**Summary:** Using skincare products as a category where consumers cannot easily verify quality before purchase, the authors run three controlled experiments across three commercial LLMs (plus a search-goods robustness check) to study how brand reputation shapes AI-mediated product recommendations. They find a "conditional monopoly" in which well-known incumbents are recommended almost every time products are otherwise identical, but this dominance collapses with as little as a 0.1-star rating edge for a rival, and can likewise be broken by exaggerated or fabricated authority-style marketing claims. The findings show LLM recommendation channels can both concentrate demand around incumbents and remain highly manipulable by marketing language, raising new competition and consumer-protection questions as AI increasingly mediates retail search.

## 4. One Capability or Many? Testing the Economic Validity of Frontier AI Evaluation [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Louis Yiven Zhu
**Date:** first posted 2026-08-29
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29420
**Summary:** Using a pre-registered latent-variable analysis of 421 model configurations across twelve benchmarks, four of them explicitly "economic," the author tests whether AI benchmarks that inform firms' purchasing decisions and regulators' scrutiny measure a distinct professional-skill capability or simply re-express general model progress. A single factor explains 74.5% of common variance across all benchmarks and tracks model release date closely, indicating that current economic AI benchmarks mostly track one general capability trend rather than differentiated professional competencies. The result is a measurement-validity warning for anyone using leaderboards to gauge firm-level AI capability, adoption readiness, or productivity potential, since apparent differentiation across models may be largely illusory.
