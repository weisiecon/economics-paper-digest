# AI Economics Papers — 2026-09-22

## 1. Understanding AI Provider Recommendations in Local Service Markets [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Hazem Ibrahim, Yasir Zaki
**Date:** 2026-09-16
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18341
**Summary:** Ibrahim and Zaki audit how AI assistants recommend doctors, financial advisers, and restaurants across the 100 largest U.S. metros, checking every AI-generated referral against official registries (Medicare clinician records, SEC adviser disclosures) under three conditions: an open-weight model, a proprietary model without web search, and the same model with search. Without search, both models mostly fabricate providers — matching a real clinician only 4-11% of the time — and recommend financial advisers with SEC misconduct disclosures at 3.6 times the registry base rate; enabling search lifts real-provider match rates to 64-71%, reverses the misconduct skew, and removes a bias toward the largest metros. The findings show that whether an AI referral is trustworthy hinges on its retrieval configuration rather than the underlying model, with direct welfare implications for how AI reshapes trust and matching in registry-backed service markets.

## 2. Whom Do AI Agents Work For? Role Assignment Induces Sponsorship Bias in LLM Recommenders [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Davood Wadi, Yu Ma
**Date:** 2026-09-16
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17989
**Summary:** Wadi and Ma study the conflict of duty facing LLM shopping agents that must evaluate sponsored listings while being deployed by platforms that profit from those placements. In controlled choice experiments, naming the platform rather than the consumer as the agent's principal in the system prompt significantly weakens the penalty and skepticism agents apply to sponsored listings, an effect that replicates across multiple LLMs and reasoning depths; a follow-up study shows stricter sponsorship labeling ("Sponsored" vs. "Promoted") reduces paid-listing choice but does not close the gap once the platform is named as principal. The results imply that disclosure rules built for human consumers do not automatically protect them once an AI agent is the one weighing the disclosure, raising a market-design question for platform competition and consumer-protection regulation as AI-mediated commerce spreads.

## 3. Algorithmic Collusion and the Complexity of Information-Value-Free Equilibria [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Ioannis Anagnostides, Weiqiang Zheng
**Date:** 2026-09-19
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22757
**Summary:** Anagnostides and Zheng study the computational complexity of "information-value-free" equilibria, a refinement proposed to detect algorithmic collusion by asking whether a player could match its recommended payoff simply by committing to a fixed action in advance. They prove that computing such an equilibrium is PPAD-complete in realistic many-player or Bayesian settings, establish an exponential query lower bound, and show that even a relaxed correlated-equilibrium version is at least as hard as solving simple stochastic games — ruling out any efficient learning dynamics regulators could lean on. The results sharply undercut a leading proposal for detecting AI-driven collusion, showing that computational intractability, not just data or legal hurdles, stands in the way of policing algorithmic pricing agents.
