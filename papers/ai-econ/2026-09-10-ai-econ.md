# AI Economics Papers — 2026-09-10

## 1. AI Innovation and Firm Performance in the Medical Device Industry [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Fazliddin Shermatov, Stephane Robin, Aldo Geuna
**Date:** 2026-09-08
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08485
**Summary:** Using FDA premarket clearances linked to USPTO patents, Scopus publications and Orbis financials, the authors build a three-stage recursive model tracing medical device firms from external AI research collaboration through AI device introduction to subsequent firm performance. External collaboration, especially with industry and clinical partners rather than academic ones, robustly drives AI device introduction and matters most for small firms substituting external ties for limited in-house R&D, and firms that bring AI devices to market show higher labor productivity that accumulates across successive device launches. Profit-margin gains are present but weaker and less robust, a pattern consistent with competitive entry eroding pricing power as AI devices diffuse through the sector, giving one of the first firm-level, causally structured answers to whether and how AI innovation actually pays off.

## 2. Why Better Models Can Create Riskier Systems: Evidence from LLM Agents in Financial Markets [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Jillian Ross, Eric So, Zoe De Simone, Charles Pozniak, Andrew W. Lo
**Date:** 2026-09-03
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04373
**Summary:** The authors build an agent-based simulation of financial markets populated by LLM trading agents of varying general-purpose capability to test whether making individual models more capable also makes the market they operate in more stable. They find that more capable frontier models behave more similarly to one another, producing correlated trading actions that do not diversify away, so higher capability reduces market-level risk when the agents' shared reasoning is accurate but turns the same correlation into a systemic liability once agents share a common misinformation environment. The resulting "capability paradox" — better individual models can worsen rather than improve aggregate market outcomes — bears directly on financial-market stability and on any market increasingly populated by AI agents.
