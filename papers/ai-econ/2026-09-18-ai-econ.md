# AI Economics Papers — 2026-09-18

## 1. Why a Non-Discriminatory Royalty Surcharge Is Not Chip-Neutral: The Error in FTC v. Qualcomm [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Sang-Seung Yi
**Date:** 2026-09-16
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18161
**Summary:** Yi revisits the Ninth Circuit's reversal in FTC v. Qualcomm, building an equilibrium model of the modem-chip market to test Qualcomm's defense that a royalty surcharge applied to every handset, regardless of chip, was "chip neutral" like a government tax. He shows the equivalence breaks down because Qualcomm, unlike the Treasury, collects the surcharge even on handsets that use a rival's chip, so raising its own price yields it a smaller gain than an equivalent tax would, and under the very conditions that make a tax neutral the surcharge inflates the rival's effective price by more than Qualcomm's own — tilting handset makers toward Qualcomm. The paper extends the result to ad valorem and discriminatory surcharges, giving antitrust economists a formal basis for reassessing the court's chip-neutrality reasoning in standard-essential-patent licensing disputes.

## 2. AI and the Market for Signals [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Doruk Cetemen, Emre Ozdenoren
**Date:** 2026-09-15
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17752
**Summary:** Cetemen and Ozdenoren extend a career-signaling model by introducing an AI industry that sells "tokens" workers can convert into output indistinguishable from their own effort, then compare equilibria with no token trade, purchases only, and open trade with side sales. They find that AI purchases raise the bar high-ability workers must clear to separate from low types, so buyers end up working less — even below the full-information benchmark — while sellers of tokens capture the AI industry's profit as the flip side of any worker welfare gain. In education-like settings where output itself has no value, every worker loses once trade opens, and standard policy levers such as token taxes, wage taxes, and watermarking have ambiguous, trade-structure-dependent effects on worker welfare.

## 3. Abundance Is an Expenditure Condition: AI Automation, Scarcity Rents, and Access [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Song Zichen
**Date:** 2026-09-12
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13801
**Summary:** Song builds a general-equilibrium model in which agents consume an automated good, a fixed-supply good, and a numeraire, to ask whether cheap AI-driven automation makes scarcity rents disappear or simply relocates them. The model proves a unique, stable equilibrium exists and derives an exact identity showing aggregate scarcity rent rises with automation if and only if real spending on the automated good falls — meaning a falling marginal cost is not sufficient for abundance if demand rebounds enough to absorb the freed-up resources. The paper further decomposes each agent's access to goods into resource-endowment and scarce-asset-ownership shares, showing redistributing one asset can worsen access inequality when ownership sources previously offset each other, and derives testable restrictions distinguishing a compute rent that can vanish from a throughput constraint whose rent persists.

## 4. Welfare-Opaque Income: Taxation under AI-Agent Delegation [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Yukun Zhang, Kemu Xu, Yishen Chen
**Date:** 2026-09-17
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20425
**Summary:** Zhang, Xu and Chen study optimal income taxation when taxpayers delegate economic decisions to an AI agent whose internal rule for translating preferences into actions is hidden from the government, creating "double unobservability" alongside the usual unobserved ability. They derive an optimal-tax condition that adds a response-weighted "execution wedge" to the standard sufficient-statistics formula, showing the same observed tax-base response can imply different welfare effects depending on whether the AI agent over- or under-executes the taxpayer's true preferences. Running 4,500 controlled trials across five commercial AI engines, they find faithful delegation reproduces the intended choice almost universally, but under conflicting objectives the engines diverge sharply in direction and magnitude — evidence that which AI model implements a taxpayer's decisions is itself a new determinant of tax policy's welfare effects.

## 5. Mitigating Emergent Collusion in LLM Pricing Agents [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Abdullah Garra
**Date:** 2026-09-11
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13037
**Summary:** Garra replicates the finding that LLM-based pricing agents can settle into supracompetitive prices in repeated oligopoly without being told to collude, reproducing the prompt-sensitivity effect from Fish et al. using DeepSeek-V3.1. He then tests three interventions against this emergent collusion — a prompt-only warning, a Harrington-inspired regulator that penalizes expected damages from supracompetitive pricing, and an active random entrant — finding the warning only dampens above-Nash pricing while the Harrington regulator closes the gap to the competitive Nash benchmark and the active entrant pushes prices below it. The results suggest AI-competition regulators aiming to prevent algorithmic collusion should target market incentives and structure directly rather than relying on prompt-level guardrails alone.
