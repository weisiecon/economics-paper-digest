# AI Economics Papers — 2026-09-09

## 1. AI Innovation and Firm Performance in the Medical Device Industry [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Fazliddin Shermatov, Stephane Robin, Aldo Geuna
**Date:** 2026-09-08
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08485
**Summary:** Using FDA premarket clearances as a directly observable, firm-attributable record of AI innovation, the authors link these to USPTO patents, Scopus publications, and Orbis financials and estimate a three-stage recursive model tracing the innovation chain from external collaboration through AI device introduction to firm outcomes. They find that external AI collaboration, especially with industry and clinical partners, is a robust driver of AI device introduction, particularly for small firms substituting external knowledge for limited in-house R&D, and that firms bringing AI devices to market see higher labor productivity that accumulates across successive device launches. Profit-margin gains are weaker and less robust, a pattern consistent with competitive entry eroding pricing power as AI devices diffuse through the sector.

## 2. From Prompt to Purchase: How AI Brand Recommendations Move Consumers on the Open Web [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Michael Iannelli, Alan Ai
**Date:** first posted 2026-06-09; revised 2026-08-31
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.10907
**Summary:** Joining opt-in clickstream data to the same users' ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini conversations, the authors isolate the causal effect of a conversational assistant naming a brand to a user with no recent engagement, using a pre-trend event study, a stance classifier, and within-response controls to strip out confounding incidental mentions. They find that a genuine brand recommendation raises the user's brand-name search, visits to the brand's own site, and retailer-page visits well above matched placebo levels, and that this response is mostly search-mediated and far larger than for incidental name-drops or unnamed competing brands in the same response. Because standard referrer-based and last-click measurement miss this upstream exposure entirely, the paper identifies a new, currently invisible channel through which conversational AI reallocates market attention and demand toward the brands it names.

## 3. AI for AI: Optimizing Additional Infrastructure Build-out to Power Artificial Intelligence Data Centers [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Alexander Crosier, Kyle Onghai, Ronnie Sircar
**Date:** 2026-09-08
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08166
**Summary:** The authors build a market-clearing framework linking data-center load growth, available generation capacity, and electricity prices, moving from a deterministic model of demand/supply growth mismatches to a stochastic model of data-center and generation build-outs, and finally to a stochastic control problem for a revenue-maximizing generation investor. They show that rapid AI-driven demand growth raises electricity prices because the supply side does not respond proportionally, and that this is not merely a forecasting failure: load-forecast uncertainty, development execution risk, and the threat of value cannibalization from overbuilding all independently weaken investors' incentive to add capacity at the pace required to keep prices stable. The result is a structural account of how AI compute demand feeds into the economics of an adjacent, capacity-constrained market.

## 4. AI worsens climate change, integrated assessment shows [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Huiying Ye, Richard S. J. Tol, Fangzhi Wang
**Date:** 2026-08-25
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24670
**Summary:** The authors extend the DICE integrated assessment model to incorporate AI's effects on emissions, output, and climate damages, distinguishing an "ICT-like" AI growth path from a more transformative "Industrial Revolution-like" path and calibrating both to available evidence. They find AI development is net polluting under current low abatement, adding 0.1°C to 2100 warming in the ICT-like scenario and 0.8°C in the IR-like scenario, with associated climate costs offsetting roughly a fifth to a quarter of AI's economic gains. Meeting a 2°C target would actually raise the optimal AI investment rate rather than crowd it out, because the trade-off between AI and abatement investment is driven primarily by AI's economic returns, not by its emissions footprint, making mitigation and AI investment complements rather than substitutes.

## 5. Why Better Models Can Create Riskier Systems: Evidence from LLM Agents in Financial Markets [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Jillian Ross, Eric So, Zoe De Simone, Charles Pozniak, Andrew W. Lo
**Date:** 2026-09-03
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04373
**Summary:** Using an agent-based simulation of LLM traders with varying capability, the authors test whether shared training and architecture make more capable models behave more similarly, creating correlated trading actions that do not diversify away as individual capability improves. They find frontier LLMs' behavior does grow more correlated with capability, that this correlation lowers market-level risk when the shared reasoning behind it is accurate, but turns into a systemic liability when agents share a common source of misinformation. The result is a "capability paradox" for financial-market stability and welfare: improving individual AI models does not necessarily improve, and can actively worsen, system-level outcomes once those models are deployed at scale as market participants.
