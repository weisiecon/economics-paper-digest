# AI Economics Papers — 2026-10-25

## 1. Causal Value Attribution of Marketing Labels in LLM-Generated Ads: A Deconfounder Approach [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Danhong You, Wei Jiang, Zhiqiang Zheng
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-16
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23025
**Summary:** As AI-generated ad creatives become a commodity, the authors argue that economic value is shifting upstream to the expert labels and configurations that steer LLM generation, and they build a deconfounder-based causal model (D-VAE) to isolate which labeling choices actually drive ad performance amid confounded, high-dimensional treatment interactions. Applied to nearly 30,000 real foundation-makeup ad creatives and validated with a field experiment, the recommended expert-label configurations raised engagement by 32.7% and performed statistically on par with human-expert-designed ads. The paper offers a concrete method for pricing and allocating value to the human expertise embedded in AI-generated content, relevant to the emerging market for AI-augmented creative and marketing skills.

## 2. Anytime Contracts for AI Services [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Boris Velasevic, Eden Saig, Eric Mazumdar
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-16
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22781
**Summary:** The authors identify a moral-hazard problem specific to AI service markets: providers can silently downgrade a query to a cheaper, lower-quality model, and the randomness of AI outputs makes this hard for users to detect, while standard contract-theory tools assume a fixed contract duration that doesn't match how users actually consume subscription and pay-as-you-go AI APIs, who can quit at any time based on what they observe. They extend principal-agent contract theory to this "anytime-exit" setting, showing that budget-optimal payment schemes can be built from statistical tests (likelihood-ratio e-values) that remain valid no matter when a user stops, and give an efficient algorithm to compute these contracts, including when the underlying models are unknown. Evaluated on synthetic data and real deployed LLMs, the framework offers a formal basis for designing AI service pricing and monitoring that deters provider cost-cutting without assuming a captive, fixed-length customer relationship.

## 3. Test-Time Compute Games [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Ander Artola Velasco, Dimitrios Rontogiannis, Stratis Tsirtsis, Manuel Gomez-Rodriguez
**Date:** first posted 2026-01-29; revised 2026-10-18
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.21839
**Summary:** The authors show that because LLM-as-a-service providers are paid in proportion to how much test-time compute they use, the market for reasoning-model inference is socially inefficient: providers have a financial incentive to run up compute spend even when it adds little to output quality, and users end up overpaying relative to the value delivered. They propose a reverse second-price auction in which providers bid a price and expected quality, and the winner is paid based on the marginal quality advantage over the runner-up bidder rather than its raw resource consumption, which they prove restores efficient incentives. Experiments with Llama, Qwen, and DeepSeek-R1-distilled models on math and science benchmarks illustrate the mechanism, pointing to a concrete pricing fix for the growing "test-time compute" segment of the AI inference market.
