# AI Economics Papers — 2026-11-19

## 1. The Economics of AI Foundation Models: Openness, Competition, and Governance [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Fasheng Xu, Xiaoyu Wang, Wei Chen, Karen Xie
**Date:** first posted 2025-10-17; revised 2026-11-17
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15200
**Summary:** This theoretical paper models an incumbent foundation-model developer's joint choice of license fee and openness over two periods, where a deployer's usage builds model-specific experience that creates a "data flywheel" favoring continued use of the incumbent against an entrant's rival model. It finds that optimal openness falls and then rises as learning strengthens, that a mandated full-openness policy or a usage subsidy can backfire by raising fees and lowering adaptation and welfare, and that vertical integration between developer and deployer raises joint profit and welfare only when learning is sufficiently strong. The results tie AI openness and competition policy directly to how pricing and integration choices interact with the portability of deployment experience across models.

## 2. When Better Artificial Intelligence Widens the Adoption Gap in Health Care [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Jonas Fooken, Ali Furkan Kalay, Metin Uyanik
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-16
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20561
**Summary:** This theoretical paper models how healthcare providers' influence over two policy levers—the share of cases switched to AI-assisted care and the number of paid reviews required alongside AI use—shapes adoption as AI capability improves. It finds that provider profits from adoption first rise and then fall as AI becomes capable enough to replace paid reviews rather than merely assist providers, so providers resist sufficiently autonomous AI and this resistance causes under-adoption relative to the patient-optimal policy, with the patient value forgone growing as AI improves. The paper shows that giving providers influence over both adoption and review requirements simultaneously widens adoption gaps and raises review burdens above what patients would prefer.

## 3. Mapping the AI Supply Chain: Dependencies, Vulnerabilities, and Market Concentration in the AI Ecosystem [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Sarah H. Cen, Lindsey A. Gailmard, Joohan Kim, Rishi Bommasani, Daniel E. Ho, Percy Liang
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-18
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24927
**Summary:** The authors build a large-scale, multilayered dataset documenting organizations and their relationships across the AI supply chain—models, data, compute, capital, and talent—compiled from public records and annotated with dates, actor types, and tags. Using this dataset they analyze network concentration, identify the most central actors, and locate chokepoints and communities within the AI ecosystem. The resulting map provides an empirical basis for studying concentration, power, and cascading-failure risk in the political economy of AI industry structure, functions the existing literature has described qualitatively but rarely measured systematically.
