# AI Economics Papers — 2026-09-07

## 1. Memoryless Algorithmic Collusion: Sure to Fail, Slow to Fall [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Zhang Xu, Wei Zhao
**Date:** first posted 2024-09-02; revised 2026-08-09
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.01147
**Summary:** In Bertrand-style pricing games, the authors show that memoryless Q-learning pricing algorithms theoretically converge to the competitive Nash equilibrium given enough exploration, but that convergence can take an extremely long time — growing super-exponentially with the discount factor — which explains why collusive-looking outcomes persist in simulations even though they are not a stable equilibrium. They develop a structural model to estimate this convergence time and uncover a non-monotonic relationship between the algorithms' learning rate and how quickly collusion breaks down, a pattern prior literature had overlooked. The result reframes observed "algorithmic collusion" as a slow-transient artifact of exploration dynamics rather than a genuine equilibrium, with direct implications for how antitrust regulators should read evidence of supracompetitive pricing among competing pricing algorithms.

## 2. Algorithm Transparency and Search Manipulation: Steering vs. Persuasion [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Raphael Boleslavsky, Thomas Jungbauer, Mehdi Shadmehr
**Date:** 2026-08-12
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12558
**Summary:** The authors build a game-theoretic model of a platform that ranks two competing products for a consumer, where its algorithm can both steer attention toward the more profitable item and, through the pattern of what it shows, convey information about which product actually fits the consumer's needs. They show that under an opaque algorithm the platform's preferred equilibrium always leads with the profitable product, but a transparent algorithm lets consumers decode what the ranking reveals, which can either deter or encourage search and can leave consumers better or worse off than under opacity depending on the setting. The results caution that algorithm-transparency mandates, a common regulatory proposal for AI-driven ranking and recommendation systems, do not uniformly help consumers because transparency simultaneously changes what the algorithm reveals and how it manipulates attention.

## 3. Algorithm Design and Physician Liability [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Shujie Luan, Shubhranshu Singh, Tinglong Dai
**Date:** 2026-08-12
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13618
**Summary:** The authors model how a U.S. liability rule — which holds physicians accountable when reliance on an AI diagnostic algorithm with unequal accuracy across patient groups contributes to a bad outcome — reshapes both the AI firm's incentive to close the accuracy gap and the physician's decision to use the algorithm at all. They find a non-monotone effect: as liability exposure rises, physicians first cut back on using AI for the disadvantaged patient group, then increase use again once the AI firm responds by investing to equalize accuracy or by switching to an equal-accuracy design. The analysis implies that a well-intentioned mandate for equal algorithmic accuracy across groups can backfire and leave both groups worse off, because it distorts the AI firm's investment incentives and the physician's equilibrium use of the tool.

## 4. AI worsens climate change, integrated assessment shows [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Huiying Ye, Richard S. J. Tol, Fangzhi Wang
**Date:** 2026-08-25
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24670
**Summary:** The authors extend the DICE integrated assessment model to jointly track how AI affects emissions, economic output and climate damages, distinguishing a modest ICT-style AI advance from a more transformative Industrial-Revolution-style advance. Calibrated to the best available evidence, they find AI development is net polluting, adding 0.1-0.8°C to warming by 2100 depending on the scenario, with the resulting climate damages offsetting roughly a fifth to a quarter of AI's economic gains. They further show the optimal trade-off between AI investment and emissions abatement is driven mainly by AI's economic growth prospects rather than by its direct emissions footprint, a distinction relevant to both climate and AI industrial policy.

## 5. Individualized Algorithmic Advice as a Strategic Signal on Competitive Markets [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Tobias R. Rebholz, Maxwell Uphoff, Christian H. R. Bernges, Florian Scholten
**Date:** first posted 2025-11-12; revised 2026-08-31
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.09454
**Summary:** In a controlled experiment, 129 participants played a Cournot quantity-competition game while receiving algorithmic production recommendations that were either aligned with the competitive equilibrium or strategically biased toward lower, collusive output levels. Recommendations biased toward collusion produced sustained underproduction and supracompetitive profits — the classic signature of tacit collusion — and participants converged toward individualized recommendations faster and more reliably than toward advice framed as collective. The findings give experimental, causal evidence that algorithmic advice tools can function as a coordination device that sustains collusion among human decision-makers even without any explicit communication between them, informing the antitrust debate over algorithmic pricing and advice systems.
