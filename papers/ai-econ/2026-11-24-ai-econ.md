# AI Economics Papers — 2026-11-24

## 1. The AI Layoff Trap [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Brett Hemenway Falk, Gerry Tsoukalas
**Date:** first posted 2026-03-21; revised 2026-11-21
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20617
**Summary:** The authors build a competitive task-based general-equilibrium model in which each firm captures the full cost savings from automating a worker's task but only internalizes a fraction of the resulting loss in aggregate consumer demand, since the rest spills onto rival firms. This demand externality locks rational, profit-maximizing firms into an automation "arms race" that displaces workers beyond the level that would maximize joint firm and worker welfare, and the excess worsens with more product-market competition and higher AI productivity. They show wage adjustment, free entry, transfers, and Coasean bargaining cannot undo the externality, but a Pigouvian tax on automation can restore the cooperative optimum, implying AI policy should target firms' competitive incentives to automate, not just the labor-market aftermath.

## 2. When AI Improves Answers but Slows Knowledge Creation: Matching and Dynamic Knowledge Creation in Digital Public Goods [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Keh-Kuan Sun
**Date:** first posted 2026-04-01; revised 2026-11-13
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00468
**Summary:** The author models a public question-and-answer platform as a dynamic public-goods game in which generative AI lets users solve problems privately instead of posting them, shrinking the stock of reusable public knowledge through both a lower posting volume and reduced follow-through from existing contributors. The model shows platforms can fall into self-sustaining "low-archive traps" where a thin knowledge base makes public answering too costly to continue, a risk that exists with or without AI but that AI narrows the viable region for avoiding. The analysis yields testable predictions that separate AI-driven private diversion from contributor exit, and suggests policy levers—logging private AI-assisted solutions back into the archive, or paying for answers—to keep public knowledge platforms from collapsing as AI use spreads.
