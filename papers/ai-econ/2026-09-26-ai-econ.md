# AI Economics Papers — 2026-09-26

## 1. Data Centers and Local Economies in the Age of AI: A Shift–Share Approach [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Fernando E. Alvarez, David Argente, Joyce Chow, Diana Van Patten
**Date:** first posted 2026-05-11; revised 2026-09-15
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35194
**Summary:** Alvarez, Argente, Chow and Van Patten build a facility-level panel of U.S. data centers linked to county-level employment, payroll, house-price and utility data, then construct a shift-share instrument from historical long-haul fiber-routing costs (following old railroad corridors) and backbone access points to separate AI-driven data-center siting from confounding local growth trends. They find that data-center expansion causally raises local employment, establishments, payroll and incomes, with construction gains concentrated early and broader employment effects persisting longer, while house prices, electricity prices and public water withdrawals all rise as well. The results show AI compute infrastructure delivers real local economic gains but at a measurable cost to housing, energy and water markets — a tradeoff for regulators weighing new data-center projects.

## 2. Rule-Based Pricing Algorithms and Market Outcomes: An Experimental Study [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Adrian Hillenbrand, Hans-Theo Normann, Matthias Potarca, Tobias Werner
**Date:** first posted 2026-09-22
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26861
**Summary:** Hillenbrand, Normann, Potarca and Werner run a controlled market experiment in which participants build rule-based pricing algorithms on a dashboard and compete in a repeated sequential Bertrand game, manipulating design features drawn from real commercial repricing tools — price-war warnings, pre-configured strategies, and advice generated by a large language model. Most of these design variations push market prices upward, an effect driven mainly by higher starting prices and more cooperative algorithm designs rather than by any single feature dominating. The findings bear directly on competition-policy debates over how platforms should be permitted to design algorithmic pricing tools, since seemingly innocuous interface choices can systematically soften price competition.

## 3. Strategic Advice in the Age of Personal AI [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Yueyang Liu, Wichinpong Park Sinchaisri
**Date:** first posted 2026-03-02; revised 2026-09-15
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02055
**Summary:** Liu and Sinchaisri model an advisor who sets a recommendation knowing that clients may separately and unpredictably consult a personal AI assistant, and derive how the advisor should optimally counteract the AI's anticipated signal as a function of how often clients consult it and how much they trust it. They show the advisor's resulting expected loss is hump-shaped in the probability of AI consultation — vanishing when AI is never or always used but peaking in between — and that greater trust in AI advice only worsens this irreducible loss, a pattern that survives once partial predictability and costly recommendation revision are added. An accompanying online forecasting experiment confirms that people blend AI advice, professional advice and their own judgment, and shows that when accessing AI requires extra effort, the decision to seek it out becomes a distinct behavioral margin from how much weight it gets once obtained.

## 4. Insuring the Fallback: Capital, Monitoring, and the Certification of Preserved Human Capability under Improving AI [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Andreas Bauer
**Date:** first posted 2026-08-09; revised 2026-09-24
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26172
**Summary:** Bauer models a professional-services firm that certifies, through a liability pledge, that it has kept enough human capability in the loop to catch an AI system's errors, where the pledge's credibility is capped by how much an underwriter will actually cover and audit. He shows the range of client stakes over which a single certificate can separate high- from low-capability providers is bounded by the provider's own capital plus only the *audited* share of the underwriter's capacity, so unmonitored ("blind") capital adds nothing to that range and instead shifts it upward through a cross-subsidy that survives only under class-based insurance rating. Monitoring, by contrast, widens the separating range, removes the cross-subsidy, and raises the payoff to genuinely preserving human skill — a formal account of how insurance-market design shapes incentives to keep humans in the loop as AI improves.

## 5. Mechanism Design Is Not Enough: Prosocial Agents for Cooperative AI [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Xuanqiang Angelo Huang, Charlie Tharas, Samuele Marro, Van Q. Truong, Bernhard Schölkopf, Emanuele La Malfa, Zhijing Jin
**Date:** first posted 2026-05-08; revised 2026-09-22
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08426
**Summary:** Huang, Tharas, Marro, Truong, Schölkopf, La Malfa and Jin ask whether mechanism design alone can align self-interested LLM agents' incentives with social welfare in repeated interaction, and prove — using incomplete-contract theory — that whenever contracts cannot specify every future contingency, a strictly positive welfare loss survives no matter how the mechanism is designed. They show that giving agents "prosocial" preferences that place some weight on others' payoffs closes this gap, producing outcomes that are simultaneously better for the group and still individually rational for each agent. Multi-agent resource-allocation experiments with LLM agents confirm the theoretical prediction, pairing a formal welfare bound with experimental evidence that AI-agent governance may need to go beyond incentive engineering alone.
