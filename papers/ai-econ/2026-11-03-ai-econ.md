# AI Economics Papers — 2026-11-03

## 1. Learning about Markets from Models: Evidence from an AI Hiring Platform [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Hossein Alidaee, Tarun Khanna, Rembrand Koning
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-31
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01226
**Summary:** Using data from an AI hiring platform that builds a separate predictive model for each client firm from a common psychometric assessment, the authors show that a firm's model is highly predictive of retention at that firm but barely better than chance at other firms, revealing stark differentiation in how firms value the same applicant traits even within a single low-skill labor market. By scoring every application against every firm's model rather than only the firm it applied to, they construct a firm-application matrix showing that an applicant's predicted fit varies enormously depending on where they apply, for two distinct, identifiable reasons. The paper argues this "model comparison externality" — visible only because the platform holds both the full applicant pool and every firm-specific model — gives platforms, firms, and policymakers a new lens for diagnosing market segmentation and targeting interventions that a single pooled model would hide.

## 2. The Market for AI-Assisted Signaling [Trade & Welfare]

**Author:** Federico Vaccari
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-02
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02910
**Summary:** This theory paper models a market in which AI providers choose the technology and price of assistance that senders use to signal ability, and shows that profitable AI assistance can reduce welfare relative to a world without it even though ability remains fully revealed, because easier imitation forces more costly signaling that outweighs the production savings AI provides. It further shows providers can profit from improving assistance aimed at lower-ability senders, since that induces higher-ability senders to buy more, again with a possible welfare loss relative to withholding the improvement. The analysis contrasts market structures: competing providers of the same technology sustain full information revelation, while development costs can instead sustain a welfare-reducing monopoly that conceals ability, and greater verification capacity can perversely reduce the information transmitted by changing which customers providers choose to serve.

## 3. A Compute Center Anyone Can Join: A Peer-to-Peer System for AI Inference [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Yimo Lin, Zhen Zhang
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-01
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01865
**Summary:** Motivated by a market in which most AI inference is bought from a handful of firms running large data centers while individually owned GPUs sit idle, the authors design the Open Inference Market, a peer-to-peer mechanism in which workers post unsettled earnings as collateral that is forfeited if spot-audited and found to have cheated. They show the number of audits needed to deter cheating is a constant per worker per day rather than scaling with throughput, so the audited share of work falls as the network grows, and that a new entrant can join with only a short burst of intensive auditing and a small bond. The mechanism's viability depends only on the ratio of the auditor's false-flag rate to its true-detection rate, giving a concrete economic path toward a decentralized alternative to concentrated compute-provider market structure.
