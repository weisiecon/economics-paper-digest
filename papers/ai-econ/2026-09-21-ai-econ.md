# AI Economics Papers — 2026-09-21

## 1. Data as Commodity: a Game-Theoretic Principle for Information Pricing [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Pasquale Casaburi, Giovanni Piccioli, Pierpaolo Vivo
**Date:** first posted 2025-10-08; revised 2026-09-15
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.07101
**Summary:** Casaburi, Piccioli and Vivo model data trading as a game among N players betting on a shared stochastic process while holding asymmetric information about past outcomes, then solve for the Nash equilibrium at which a better-informed player chooses to sell part of her data to rivals. They show the resulting price range departs sharply from standard supply-and-demand intuition: informed players can profit from selling to the uninformed, data can change hands even at a price of zero without hurting the seller, competition among informed traders can benefit the uninformed, and trades infeasible in small markets become viable as the market grows. The paper gives economists a tractable, game-theoretic starting point for pricing a genuinely non-rival, near-zero-marginal-cost commodity, with direct bearing on how AI firms and data brokers should price training data.

## 2. Understanding AI Provider Recommendations in Local Service Markets [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Hazem Ibrahim, Yasir Zaki
**Date:** first posted 2026-09-16
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18341
**Summary:** Ibrahim and Zaki audit AI assistants acting as referral agents for doctors, financial advisers and restaurants across the 100 largest U.S. metro areas, checking every recommendation against official registries (Medicare, SEC) under three retrieval conditions. Without web search, both an open-weight and a proprietary model mostly fabricate providers that don't exist in the named city, and the advisers they do recommend carry SEC misconduct records at 3.6 times the base rate; adding search lifts real-provider match rates to 64-71%, cuts misconduct exposure below the registry average, and erases a bias toward recommending providers in the largest metros. The results show the market value and safety of AI-mediated referrals hinge entirely on retrieval configuration rather than model quality, illustrating a concrete information-asymmetry and quality-verification failure in an AI-intermediated market.

## 3. Strategic Advice in the Age of Personal AI [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Yueyang Liu, Wichinpong Park Sinchaisri
**Date:** first posted 2026-03-02; revised 2026-09-15
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02055
**Summary:** Liu and Sinchaisri model an advisor who must set a recommendation knowing the client may also consult a predictable personal-AI assistant, characterizing how the advisor's optimal counter-positioning and resulting expected loss vary with the probability the client checks the AI and how much weight the client places on it. They find the advisor's loss is hump-shaped in consultation probability, vanishing only when the AI is consulted never or always, while greater client trust in the AI raises the loss from this stochastic consultation; extensions cover partial predictability, costly adjustment, and cases where the AI holds private information. A companion forecasting experiment confirms participants blend the advisor's, the AI's, and their own judgment, but friction in accessing AI advice causes some to skip or fail to obtain it — showing that the mere availability of personal AI reshapes the economics of professional advice-giving.
