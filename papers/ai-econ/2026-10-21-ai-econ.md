# AI Economics Papers — 2026-10-21

## 1. The Macroeconomic Effect of AI: Sizing the Software Engineering Channel [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Alex Blumenfeld, Jonathon Hazell, Chen Lian, Andreas Schaab
**Date:** first posted 2026-09-28
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35793
**Summary:** The authors build a forward-looking, real-time measure of AI's productivity impact by estimating how sensitive each firm's stock return is to an AI stock-market index, and how that sensitivity scales with the firm's share of payroll in software engineering, then use a model to translate this cross-sectional asset-pricing pattern into productivity gains. From November 2022 to December 2025 they find AI raised the market's expected present value of software engineering productivity by the equivalent of a permanent 32.6% increase, implying a GDP level effect of 3.6% in the baseline (6.5% if higher software productivity also raises R&D productivity). They further show that by mid-2026, as coding agents improved rapidly, the estimated effect on productivity and GDP had more than doubled relative to late 2025, suggesting the macroeconomic impact of AI is still accelerating rather than leveling off.

## 2. Do Markets Dream of Artificial Intelligence? Macroeconomic Effects and International Spillovers of AI News Shocks [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Maria Sole Pagliari
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-01
**Source:** https://www.dnb.nl/en/publications/research-publications/working-paper-2026/871-do-markets-dream-of-artificial-intelligence-macroeconomic-effects-and-international-spillovers-of-ai-news-shocks/
**Summary:** Pagliari identifies "AI news shocks" — revisions in markets' expectations about AI's economic value — using a daily equity factor built on firms' differing AI exposure, combined with sign, magnitude and narrative restrictions in a Bayesian VAR, then traces their macroeconomic effects in the US and euro area. In the US the adjustment is investment-led: capital-goods orders rise early and persistently while aggregate activity responds more slowly, producer prices rise before consumer prices, and the expansion spreads outward from AI-linked production-network sectors; in the euro area, equity prices rise, the euro depreciates, and import and energy costs push up inflation before real activity strengthens. The results point to a monetary-policy dilemma, since financial spillovers and imported price pressures from AI news arrive well before the broader productivity gains do.

## 3. Liability and Pricing of Dual-Use AI [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Joshua S. Gans
**Date:** first posted 2026-09-28
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35828
**Summary:** Gans models how much liability an AI provider should bear when the same service can be used for both attack and defense, with providers selling a common input to productive users, attackers and defenders alike. He shows that a higher common price can reduce defensive effort without changing attack success, that compensating victims weakens defense and raises attacker profits, and that the welfare-optimal liability rule must balance these security effects against the risk of excluding productive users — with greater competition among providers generally lowering the optimal liability a monopolist would otherwise bear. Where "guardrails" that preserve productive uses are technically available, competition favors universal guarding and can make liability rules redundant, while under monopoly a unique liability threshold governs adoption and zero liability is sometimes optimal, giving regulators a concrete framework for setting AI liability rules rather than treating "more liability" as always safer.

## 4. Circular relationships among AI firms [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Jon Frost, Rudraksh Kansal, Kumar Rishabh, Vatsala Shreeti, Leanne Si Ying Zhang
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-01
**Source:** https://www.bis.org/publications/bulletin-137-circular-relationships-among-ai-firms
**Summary:** Matching deal-level investment data for 1,246 AI firms across five supply-chain layers (compute, infrastructure, data tools, models and applications) against commercial-relationship data, the authors document that 28.7% of AI firms' investment deals by value (2021–2025) targeted another AI firm, and 55.2% of incoming investment into AI firms came from other AI firms. Of AI-to-AI deals, 16.1% by count and 46.4% by value also involved a commercial supply-chain relationship between investor and target, meaning the same firms that invest in each other frequently buy from or sell to each other too. The authors argue these circular structures reflect genuine economic motives — securing scarce inputs and mitigating information asymmetries — but also concentrate macroeconomic risk and reduce transparency about who ultimately bears the risk if AI valuations fall.

## 5. Everyone Is a Manager: AI Use as an Act of Management [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Benjamin T. Leyden, Daniela Scur
**Date:** first posted 2026-09-30
**Source:** https://ideas.repec.org/p/ces/ceswps/_13030.html
**Summary:** The authors argue that when a knowledge worker gets an AI assistant, they stop executing tasks and start delegating them, so using AI is itself a managerial act, and they model the resulting productivity return as governed by two managerial skills: direction (specifying what to delegate well) and verification (catching the tool's errors). As AI reliability improves, the skill with the higher marginal payoff shifts from verification toward direction; because direction keeps building with use but verification does not, and because workers who bear only part of the cost of their own errors reach this point sooner, verification skill tends to atrophy as AI improves. In equilibrium the strongest verifiers sort into the jobs where losing that skill would be most costly, offering a management-economics explanation for why otherwise similar firms and workers see such heterogeneous returns from adopting the same AI tools.
