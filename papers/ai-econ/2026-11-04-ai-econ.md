# AI Economics Papers — 2026-11-04

## 1. Prizes, Shared Hazard, and the Speed of Frontier AI [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Li Gan
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-03
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04266
**Summary:** This theory paper models frontier AI labs as competing in a winner-take-most contest for commercial leadership while each lab bears only part of the catastrophe risk ("hazard externality") that its own speed adds to the whole industry. In a calibrated three-lab takeoff model, the contest for leadership alone explains the large majority of the gap between racing speed and a coordinated, safer pace, while adding more competing labs raises each lab's individual incentive to race without changing the industry's total hazard under a common research-sharing rule. The paper argues that voluntary measures such as the September 2026 White House Accord on Super Intelligence, which audit safety without capping speed, leave each lab's incentive to race untouched and so fail to slow the race in the model.

## 2. Agreements Have a Clock: Compute Growth and the Verification of AI Restraint [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Li Gan
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-03
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04272
**Summary:** Drawing an analogy to nuclear, ozone, and climate agreements, this theory paper asks what it takes for rival powers to sustain an agreement to pause frontier AI development, modeling a follower that catches up through its own research plus diffusion from the paused leader, with diffusion fading as the technology gap narrows. Cooperation is sustainable as long as the cost of monitoring needed to deter a secret breakout stays below what each side gains from cooperating, but because compute keeps growing during a pause, a secret advance can reach the frozen frontier sooner as time passes, steadily shrinking the viable cooperation window. In an illustrative calibration, the agreement holds only through month 18.9, extending to 25.5 months with verified safety-research sharing and to 31.8 months if monitoring costs are halved, implying that AI restraint agreements need continual reinforcement rather than a one-time deal.

## 3. When Better Opportunities Backfire: Service Worker Responses to Parallel AI Deployment in Customer Acquisition [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Xinyu Fu, Shulong Luo, Narayan Ramasubbu, Peng Shen, Xincheng Wang, Yucheng Wang
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-19
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25603
**Summary:** Using more than half a million outreach records from a Chinese online health-insurance brokerage that deployed an AI voice agent to handle low-intent leads while routing higher-intent leads to humans, the authors use a regression-discontinuity-in-time design to trace how this parallel AI deployment reshapes human worker behavior across the workday. Early in the day, when AI-driven lead enrichment raises the odds that a call is high-intent by about 23 percentage points, human customer acquisition on those same calls falls by about 5 percentage points, because workers anticipating better leads later raise their effective reservation value and persist less with the lead in hand, reallocating leads more often and cutting calls short. A calibrated decomposition shows this behavioral response outweighs the efficiency gain from better lead ordering, indicating that firms deploying AI alongside human workers must manage how opportunities are distributed over the workday, not just overall workload, to capture productivity gains.
