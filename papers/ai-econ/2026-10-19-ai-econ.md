# AI Economics Papers — 2026-10-19

## 1. The Macroeconomic Effect of AI: Sizing the Software Engineering Channel [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Alex Blumenfeld, Jonathon Hazell, Chen Lian, Andreas Schaab
**Date:** first posted 2026-09-24
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35793
**Summary:** The authors build a forward-looking measure of AI's productivity impact from financial markets, estimating each firm's stock-return sensitivity to an AI-exposure index and how that sensitivity scales with the firm's share of payroll devoted to software engineers, then feed the cross-sectional pattern into a structural model to back out implied software-engineering productivity gains. From November 2022 through December 2025 they estimate AI raised the present value of software-engineering productivity by an amount equivalent to a permanent 32.6% productivity increase, translating into a 3.6% boost to the level of GDP in their baseline, rising to 6.5% if the gains also spill over into R&D productivity. They find the estimated GDP effect more than doubled by mid-2026 as coding-agent capabilities advanced rapidly, showing financial markets are already pricing in a sizable AI-driven productivity transition concentrated in one specific occupational channel.

## 2. Do Markets Dream of Artificial Intelligence? Macroeconomic Effects and International Spillovers of AI News Shocks [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Maria Sole Pagliari
**Date:** first posted September 2026
**Source:** https://ideas.repec.org/p/dnb/dnbwpp/871.html
**Summary:** Pagliari identifies AI "news shocks" using a daily equity factor built from firms' heterogeneous exposure to AI, then traces their macroeconomic consequences in the US and euro area with a Bayesian VAR. In the US, capital-goods orders rise quickly and persist while broader economic activity responds more slowly, and producer prices rise ahead of consumer prices as the boom spreads outward from AI-exposed sectors. In the euro area the same shocks raise equity prices and depreciate the euro, importing cost pressures that lift inflation expectations and interest rates before any productivity payoff materializes — creating what the author calls a monetary-policy dilemma, since AI's financial spillovers and price pressures arrive well before its productivity gains do.

## 3. Circular relationships among AI firms [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Jon Frost, Rudraksh Kansal, Kumar Rishabh, Vatsala Shreeti, Leanne Si Ying Zhang
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-01
**Source:** https://www.bis.org/publications/bulletin-137-circular-relationships-among-ai-firms
**Summary:** Mapping deal-level investment data across 1,246 AI companies spanning compute, infrastructure, data, models and applications, the authors document that 55.2% of all capital flowing into AI firms between 2021 and 2025 came from other AI firms, and that 28.7% of AI firms' own deal value went to targets that were themselves AI companies. Nearly half of these intra-AI deals by value also involved a commercial supply relationship between the same two parties, suggesting the ties are driven by firms securing scarce compute, data or model inputs and guarding against hold-up risk rather than by ordinary financial diversification. The authors argue this circularity concentrates risk and reduces transparency about the AI sector's true capital base, a market-structure vulnerability regulators and investors should weigh when assessing the AI investment boom.

## 4. Liability and Pricing of Dual-Use AI [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Joshua S. Gans
**Date:** first posted 2026-09-24
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35828
**Summary:** Gans models an AI provider that sells a common input used by productive users, attackers and defenders alike, asking how liability for misuse-driven harm should be set and how it interacts with pricing. He shows a higher common price can improve security by discouraging low-value defensive effort without changing attack success, while compensating victims directly tends to weaken defense and raise attackers' profits, so the welfare-optimal liability level trades these effects off against the cost of excluding legitimate users. The model implies a monopolist will choose only partial liability, that stronger competition can lower the optimal liability level but never eliminate defense entirely, and that when effective guardrails exist, a universal-guarding requirement can make liability rules unnecessary altogether — offering a quantitative framework for how dual-use AI should be regulated and priced.

## 5. More than just Plug and Play: Early Evidence on Organisational Capital and AI Adoption [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Diane Coyle, Nghi Nguyen, John Lourenze Poquiz, Rebecca Riley
**Date:** first posted September 2026
**Source:** https://ideas.repec.org/p/eoe/escoed/escoe-dp-2026-13.html
**Summary:** Using panel data from the UK's Management and Expectations Survey, the authors test whether firms' existing management practices predict which ones adopt AI, and whether that relationship differs from adoption of other technologies like robotics or specialized software. They find firms with stronger baseline management — particularly around performance measurement — are significantly more likely to take up AI, while the same management capabilities show no comparable link to adopting robotics, equipment or software, indicating that AI adoption depends on organisational complementarities in a way distinct from earlier technology waves. The result implies that policies aimed at spurring broader AI adoption need to address firm-level management capacity specifically, since the bottleneck is organisational readiness rather than plug-and-play availability.
