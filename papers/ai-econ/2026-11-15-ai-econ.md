# AI Economics Papers — 2026-11-15

## 1. Competition in Generative AI: Evidence from Consumer Demand [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Mark L. Egan, Dominic Russel, Claire Shi, Vincent Yao
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-22
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35953 (NBER Working Paper 35953)
**Summary:** Using transaction-level card data covering 174 million U.S. consumers and 114 billion transactions, the authors document that only about 2.9% of consumers paid for a generative AI service as of April 2026, skewed toward younger, higher-income users on sub-$30/month plans. A structural model of demand and supply finds consumers are highly price-sensitive and more loyal to "AI" as a category than to any specific provider, with switching clustering around major model releases and annual retention of just 20-45%. The results imply that unless model quality becomes far more differentiated, this substitutability will cap providers' pricing power and let consumers keep most of the surplus from generative AI competition.

## 2. Designing AI-Mediated Communication in Teams [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Wichinpong Park Sinchaisri, Zhiwei Wang
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-03
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04686
**Summary:** This theoretical paper models how a firm should design AI-mediated communication between two workers with conflicting interests and the firm's own objectives, where an AI assistant knows aggregate project conditions and the firm chooses how much of that information to disclose before workers report their private local conditions. It finds that under weak alignment between workers and the firm, coarsening the disclosed information preserves truthful preference reporting, whereas under strong alignment the firm prefers full disclosure because preference reports otherwise invite costly accommodation of workers' private agendas, with delayed context release or worker-controlled forwarding able to sustain honest communication further. The results show that the economic value of integrating AI assistants into firm coordination hinges on how information-disclosure timing interacts with internal incentive conflicts, not on the technology alone.

## 3. Information Aggregation in Markets with Analysts, Experts, and Chatbots [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Wolfgang Kuhle
**Date:** first posted 2024-11-04; revised 2026-11-08
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.01938
**Summary:** This theoretical finance paper extends a noisy-rational-expectations model of information aggregation, in which traders interpret public news such as analyst reports differently, to include AI chatbots that offer free financial advice while learning about an asset's fundamental value from users' queries. It finds that chatbots aggregate information more efficiently than market prices alone, increase trading volume, and raise the informational content of prices, but that in doing so the AI platform itself accumulates an absolute informational advantage over every other market participant. The result points to a structural shift in price discovery away from markets and toward AI platforms, raising distributional questions about who captures the resulting information rents.
