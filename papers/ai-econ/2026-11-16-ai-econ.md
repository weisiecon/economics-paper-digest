# AI Economics Papers — 2026-11-16

## 1. Generative AI, Managerial Expectations, and Economic Activity [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Manish Jha, Jialin Qian, Michael Weber, Baozhong Yang
**Date:** first posted 2024-10-04; revised 2026-11-09
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03897
**Summary:** The authors use generative AI to extract managers' economic expectations from more than 120,000 corporate earnings-call transcripts, building firm-, industry-, and economy-wide "AI Economy Scores." These scores predict GDP growth, production, employment, and wages five or more quarters ahead, improving on survey-based forecasts, with the predictive power concentrated among historically more accurate and less-biased managers. The findings suggest AI-extracted managerial sentiment carries real information about future economic activity rather than noise, offering a new high-frequency input for macroeconomic forecasting.

## 2. Bridging Distant Ideas: the Impact of AI on R&D and Recombinant Innovation [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Emanuele Bazzichi, Massimo Riccaboni, Fulvio Castellacci
**Date:** first posted 2026-04-02; revised 2026-11-13
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02189
**Summary:** This paper develops a Schumpeterian quality-ladder model in which firms recombine ideas across varying "distances" in knowledge space while AI automates a growing share of R&D tasks. It finds that the effect of AI automation on innovation strategy is non-monotonic: firms initially pursue more radical, distant recombinations, but beyond a threshold shift back toward incremental innovation, producing a hump-shaped path for aggregate knowledge growth. It further shows that the innovation distance firms choose in decentralized equilibrium can be socially suboptimal, pointing to a welfare-relevant gap between private and social returns to AI-assisted R&D.

## 3. The Price of Intelligence: A Quality-Adjusted Price Index for AI Services [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Louis Yiven Zhu
**Date:** first posted 2026-08-30; revised 2026-11-02
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29843
**Summary:** The author builds quality-adjusted price indices for AI text and code inference APIs by matching over 21,000 posted prices to a capability scale derived from thousands of benchmark scores. A pre-specified hedonic index shows prices falling roughly 51% a year in log terms once rising model capability is accounted for, far faster than the roughly 7% annual decline implied by naive same-model comparisons, though the exact rate is sensitive to anchor-model choices and possible benchmark contamination. The results show that headline claims about AI getting cheaper depend heavily on index methodology, much as in standard hedonic price measurement for other high-tech industries.

## 4. AI Search as an Information Intermediary: Clickstream Evidence on Web Traffic [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Qiaoni Shi, Kai Gu, Kai Zhu
**Date:** first posted 2026-07-08; revised 2026-11-12
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07652
**Summary:** Using U.S. desktop clickstream data around the rollout of ChatGPT Search, the authors compare how often AI search versus traditional search engines refer users onward to the open web. They find ChatGPT Search sends far fewer referrals than Google (5.4% of sessions versus 31.1% of queries), favors reference and technical sites over ad-supported ones, and that conventional search usage declines as ChatGPT adoption spreads, especially among users without prior accounts. The results indicate AI search is reshaping the economics of web traffic and content referral, with implications for ad-supported publishers and the broader market structure of online information provision.

## 5. Monetizing Intelligence: Advertising and Subscription Design on LLM Platforms [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Yuhang Ji, Fasheng Xu, Tianjun Feng
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-23
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32945
**Summary:** The authors model a monopolist LLM platform that jointly sets response quality, advertising intensity, and subscription prices across ad-supported and ad-free tiers, where ads lower user value but higher quality raises advertising returns. They show an ad-supported tier can fund service for users who would otherwise get none, but it also lets the platform raise ad-free prices by degrading the ad-supported alternative, producing "utility polarization" even as access broadens, while a moderate cap on advertising intensity can maximize consumer surplus. They further show cheaper compute can raise quality and cut per-user ad load while still increasing total advertising revenue, because better service raises the value of each remaining ad impression.
