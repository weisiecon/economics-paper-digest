# AI Economics Papers — 2026-09-24

## 1. The Economics of Recursive Self-Improvement [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Tom Cunningham, Lukas Althoff, Basil Halperin, Brian Jabarian, Andrew Koh, Arjun Ramani, Phil Trammell, Parker Whitfill, Cheryl Wu
**Date:** first posted 2026-09-14
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15802
**Summary:** Cunningham, Althoff, Halperin, Jabarian, Koh, Ramani, Trammell, Whitfill and Wu build a sequence of directed-graph models of AI progress to formalize the feedback loops behind recursive self-improvement, showing that net acceleration in capabilities depends on the product of elasticities across each loop. They distinguish AI systems that merely get better at narrow AI-R&D benchmarks from those that improve at broadly valuable economic tasks, and calibrate their framework against existing data alongside a wish list of parameters firms could measure and share publicly. Their back-of-envelope calibration finds the feedback loops are not yet strong enough to produce a self-sustaining acceleration, though they appear to be strengthening, and the paper closes by weighing how plausible and consequential such an acceleration could become.

## 2. Why a Non-Discriminatory Royalty Surcharge Is Not Chip-Neutral: The Error in FTC v. Qualcomm [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Sang-Seung Yi
**Date:** first posted 2026-09-16
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18161
**Summary:** Yi builds an equilibrium model of the modem-chip market to test Qualcomm's Ninth-Circuit-accepted defense that its per-handset royalty surcharge was "chip neutral" because it did not vary with which chip a handset used. He shows the facial neutrality is illusory: unlike an equivalent government tax, which is simply foregone on lost sales, Qualcomm keeps collecting its surcharge even on handsets using a rival's chip, so raising its own price costs it less under the surcharge than under a tax, letting Qualcomm push up the rival's price — and cut the rival's output — by more than its own. For ad valorem surcharges the "chip neutral" defense fails for a different reason (it presumes a zero FRAND royalty rate), giving a formal economic rebuttal to a legal precedent that will shape how courts assess royalty-based exclusionary conduct in the semiconductor industry.

## 3. AI and the Market for Signals [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Doruk Cetemen, Emre Ozdenoren
**Date:** first posted 2026-09-15
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17752
**Summary:** Cetemen and Ozdenoren add an AI industry that sells "tokens" — capable of producing output indistinguishable from a worker's own — into a classic career-signaling model of early-career labor markets. Comparing a world with no token trade, one where only lower-ability workers buy tokens, and one with open trade including side sales, they show AI purchases by low types raise the bar high types must clear, pushing high-ability workers to work harder while buyers work less, and that with cheap, freely tradeable tokens the signal stops depending on effort altogether and every type under-invests in skill. Welfare gains from AI token trade are possible but not guaranteed — workers gain only when overall welfare rises — and standard remedies such as token taxes, wage taxes, and watermarking have ambiguous, context-dependent effects on both payoffs and welfare.

## 4. Sophistication in GenAI Use: Field Evidence from a Large Firm [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Nicholas J. Hallman, Zachary T. Kowaleski, Anu Puvvada, Jaime J. Schmidt
**Date:** first posted 2026-08-27
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27364
**Summary:** Hallman, Kowaleski, Puvvada and Schmidt use proprietary data on 713,564 employee prompts and LLM responses from nearly 4,000 back-office employees across 15 functional areas of a large firm over eight months in 2025 to measure how sophisticated generative-AI use varies within an organization. They find senior employees use genAI more sophisticatedly, consistent with domain expertise complementing AI capability, and that sophistication peaks in functions focused on firmwide strategy and organizational change such as Strategy, Digital Innovation, and Project Management. Sophistication neither improves organically over time nor responds durably to formal AI training, suggesting effective genAI use is a persistent skill gap rather than something firms can quickly train away, with direct implications for how managers approach internal AI adoption.

## 5. The Network Advantage: Benefits of Interregional Scientific Knowledge Spillovers on AI Patenting [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Saverio Barabuffi, Jacopo Cricchio, Alberto Di Minin, Guido Pialli
**Date:** first posted 2026-09-22
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26604
**Summary:** Barabuffi, Cricchio, Di Minin and Pialli use a network-econometric approach across 26 Chinese provincial regions from 2006–2020 to ask whether interregional knowledge spillovers carried through co-authored AI research — rather than the co-invention ties usually studied — raise a region's AI patenting output. They find that co-publication spillovers do foster local AI patenting, extending evidence on deliberate scientific networks beyond the geographic-proximity externalities usually emphasized in the innovation literature. The returns to these spillovers concentrate almost entirely in regions where privately owned enterprises dominate regional R&D, meaning the same interregional knowledge flows reinforce rather than narrow regional divergence in AI innovation capacity.
