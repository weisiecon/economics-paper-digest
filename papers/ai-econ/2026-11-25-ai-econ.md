# AI Economics Papers — 2026-11-25

## 1. Organizing Intelligence [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Alex Imas, Andrew Koh, Shane Legg
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-31
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05417
**Summary:** The authors build a model in which human and AI agents with multidimensional capabilities sort endogenously into competing firms, separating competence (which raises productivity levels) from coordination (which governs how productivity scales with firm size). Because AI expertise is reproducible while human talent is fixed in supply, and AI may not share humans' coordination limits, rising AI competence first changes which workers staff a firm and can shrink average firm size, but once AI coordination improves enough it breaks the "Coasean ceiling" and the market structure undergoes a phase transition toward a few large, AI-heavy firms. The model decomposes resulting wage changes into a technology effect and a reorganization effect that can run in opposite directions, implying that how AI transforms the economy depends as much on AI's ability to coordinate organizations as on its raw capability.

## 2. Burning the Bridge: Agentification and Domestic Value Added [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Eduardo Levy Yeyati
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-23
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31518
**Summary:** The author models a small open economy in which both exporters and local-service firms can replace domestic workers with foreign AI agents, raising output per paid hour but shifting income abroad once wages reach a reservation floor. Calibrating to Brazilian and Mexican national accounts, the paper finds that once local suppliers capture a bargaining share above roughly 0.15, further domestic adoption of foreign agents in local services reduces domestic value added and consumption even at negligible foreign costs, though exporters can offset this by using agents to expand output and earn the foreign exchange needed to pay for them. It derives the export-growth rates required to preserve income or consumption under agent adoption and warns that eroding domestic capabilities over time can raise the future cost of reshoring production, strengthening suppliers' bargaining position at contract renewal.

## 3. Binding Constraints in a Strategic Technology: An Industrial Vulnerability Index for the Foundation Model Industry [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Claudio Pirrone, Stefano Fricano, Gioacchino Fazio
**Date:** first posted 2025-10-27; revised 2026-11-24
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.23421
**Summary:** The authors construct the Artificial Intelligence Industrial Vulnerability Index (AIIVI), a composite of fourteen concentration and sustainability indicators spanning compute, energy, capital, talent, and data, aggregated with an O-ring function so the single most-constrained input dominates overall exposure. Applied to the nine developers at the capability frontier as of August 2026, the index comes in at 0.62-0.75 depending on weighting, with compute and energy access identified as by far the largest drivers of vulnerability, well ahead of capital, talent, or data. The paper argues that AI industrial policy, which concentrates public investment in compute while largely neglecting grid interconnection and generation, may be misallocated relative to where frontier AI production is actually most exposed, and flags under-addressed risks from data-centre debt financing, local siting opposition, and export controls on deployed models rather than hardware.

## Excluded, borderline

- **Information Games: Strategic Crowding and Firm Repositioning in Language-Model Space** (arXiv:2609.37820) — uses LLM-derived text embeddings to measure general firm competitive dynamics; the paper is about using AI as a measurement method, not about the economics of AI markets.
- **Useful Work Can Increase Energy Use: Demand Elasticity and Proof of Useful Work** (arXiv:2611.13131) — a clean demand-elasticity argument about blockchain mining subsidies; AI (matrix-multiplication-based Proof-of-Useful-Work) is only one illustrative instantiation, not the paper's subject.
- **The Digital-Energy Recomposition of the Kitchin Cycle** (arXiv:2610.33122) — macro business-cycle analysis of IT investment and data-centre electricity, but authors flag it as exploratory on very small AI-era samples (N=16 for the Irish series) and partly attribute their own headline correlation to a tax-structure artifact.
- **From the firm- to the application-level: A new typology of AI use in organisations** (arXiv:2611.15371) — a conceptual taxonomy of firm AI use with no causal design, structural model, or market outcome.
- **The Fake Friend Dilemma: Relational Trust and the Political Economy of Conversational AI** (arXiv:2601.03222) — a normative/conceptual essay on trust and conversational AI, not a formal economic analysis.
- **The Rise of Agents and the Labor Market** (arXiv:2611.27210) and **AI-Assisted Programming and Labor Demand** (arXiv:2611.31083) — rigorous firm-level evidence on agentic AI and job postings/hiring, but primarily about labor demand; left to the ai-labor digest (the latter already appears there).
- **The Welfare Limits of Market Segmentation with Captive Consumers** (arXiv:2610.39139) — a well-posed duopoly welfare result, but has no connection to AI; a false positive from the antitrust/welfare keyword search.
