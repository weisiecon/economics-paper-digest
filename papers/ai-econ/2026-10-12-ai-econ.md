# AI Economics Papers — 2026-10-12

## 1. Who Keeps the Gains from Personal AI Assistants? Seller Adaptation and the Unassisted in a Language-Model Market Simulation [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Haonan Huang, Joey Xiao
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-05
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.05823
**Summary:** Huang and Xiao build an agent-based rental market where language models play consumers, AI shopping assistants, and six adaptive sellers who respond with algorithmic pricing tools, comparing outcomes when sellers are frozen versus allowed to adapt their fees and headline rates. They find that executing AI assistants initially cut adopters' spending substantially, but adaptive sellers claw back roughly a third of those savings by raising listed rates while cutting the fees assistants target, and the behaviorally calibrated forecast of harm to unassisted consumers fails to materialize once sellers adapt. The study argues that the welfare effects of personal AI assistants can only be judged at the market level, including seller responses and non-adopters, not from single-user studies of the assistant alone.

## 2. Convergence to Collusion in Algorithmic Pricing [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Kevin Michael Frick
**Date:** first posted 2026-04-17; revised 2026-10-01
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.15825
**Summary:** Frick trains average-reward soft actor-critic reinforcement learning algorithms to set continuous prices in a repeated Bertrand duopoly with symmetric firms, logit demand and only price and profit feedback, directly testing how quickly and reliably algorithmic pricing can learn to collude. Within 50,000 periods the algorithms can reach collusive outcomes, and in 36% of simulations they learn reward-punishment schemes that make undercutting unprofitable, sustaining prices above the competitive level; in the remaining cases, deviations remain profitable by only hundredths of a percent, making the collusive equilibrium fragile. The results give competition authorities a quantitative benchmark for how fast and how robustly common pricing-algorithm architectures converge to tacit collusion absent any explicit coordination.

## 3. Pricing Time, Not Just Tokens: Latency-Aware Mechanism Design for LLM Inference [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Ian McDougall, Karthikeyan Sankaralingam
**Date:** first posted 2026-09-30; revised 2026-10-02
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.40098
**Summary:** McDougall and Sankaralingam model LLM inference as a service market where buyers have private willingness-to-pay, task volume and time preference, and derive how a seller should price latency alongside token counts using GPU hardware's actual compute-bound and bandwidth-bound cost structure. Their main theoretical result is a separation theorem showing that offering a small number of discrete hardware tiers lets buyers self-select on time preference, collapsing a hard three-dimensional screening problem into simple one-dimensional screening within each tier, and that optimal per-task prices end up independent of volume — a theoretical justification for the flat per-token pricing seen in real APIs. Calibrating the model to actual H100 and B200 GPU clusters, they show the separation result holds in the large majority of realistic configurations and that two-tier pricing captures 26-66% more profit than the best single-tier price.

## 4. AI-Mediation in Retail Markets: Revenue Effects and Assortment Optimization [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Omar El Housni, Marouane Ibn Brahim
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-09
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12917
**Summary:** El Housni and Ibn Brahim study how a retailer should plan its product assortment when an AI shopping assistant forms a customer-facing shortlist before a shopper applies a logit choice model to it, comparing a fixed segment-specific AI ranking rule against a stochastic Plackett-Luce ranking estimated from real AI-model product rankings. They find that ignoring AI mediation and planning assortments as if customers see the full catalog costs retailers between 2.5% and 11.4% of revenue, while a simple rule that re-ranks by the AI's fitted utilities recovers almost all of that loss, and they further show the resulting assortment-optimization problem remains tractable for a fixed number of customer segments. The paper gives retailers and competition regulators a concrete, quantified picture of how AI intermediaries reshape firms' optimal catalogs and revenue.

## 5. Risk Ceilings and Development Deadlines: Pacing AI under Uncertain Safety Productivity [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Li Gan
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-08
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11093
**Summary:** Gan models a regulator who wants to commit simultaneously to an upper bound on a future AI model's hazard and to a fixed development deadline, when the productivity of safety research is not known in advance, asking whether both commitments are jointly credible. He shows that sequencing development so that safety-relevant learning happens first and is then "replayed" with spare compute devoted to safety comes close to the minimum safety-productivity threshold needed to honor both promises at once, requiring only about 3.3% more productivity than the theoretical bound in his baseline calibration. The analysis implies that rules fixing how compute is allocated across capability and safety research effectively fix delivery dates rather than risk levels, a distinction relevant to designing credible AI development regulation.
