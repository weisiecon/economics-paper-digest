# AI Economics Papers — 2026-11-05

## 1. The Price of Intelligence: A Quality-Adjusted Price Index for AI Services [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Louis Yiven Zhu
**Date:** first posted 2026-08-30; revised 2026-11-02
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29843
**Summary:** This paper constructs quality-adjusted price indices for AI text and code inference sold through APIs by matching 21,024 posted prices to a capability scale built from 4,605 benchmark scores, rather than relying on simple matched-model comparisons that ignore quality change. Naive year-over-year comparisons show inference prices falling only slowly (about 7% a year in log terms), but the author's preferred hedonic index — which credits rising model capability for part of the price change — implies a much faster quality-adjusted decline of roughly 51% a year, though the estimate swings from 34% to 89% depending on how measurement error and possible benchmark contamination are treated. The paper cautions that while different methods agree closely on how AI providers rank against each other, they disagree sharply on the level and pace of quality-adjusted price declines, so headline claims about AI getting "cheaper per unit of intelligence" hinge heavily on index construction.

## 2. The Market for AI-Assisted Signaling [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Federico Vaccari
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-02
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02910
**Summary:** This theory paper models a market in which AI providers choose both the technology and the price of assistance that individuals use to signal their ability to evaluators, in a setting where who actually buys the assistance is unobserved. It shows that profit-maximizing AI assistance can reduce overall welfare relative to a world without such tools, even though ability remains fully revealed in equilibrium, because easier imitation forces higher-ability senders to produce more output to stay distinguishable, and this extra signaling cost can outweigh the production savings the AI provides. The model further shows that competition among providers of the same technology tends to preserve full information, but that development costs can sustain a monopoly provider that instead conceals ability, and that expanding verification capacity can backfire by changing which customers a provider chooses to serve.

## 3. Learning about Markets from Models: Evidence from an AI Hiring Platform [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Hossein Alidaee, Tarun Khanna, Rembrand Koning
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-31
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01226
**Summary:** Using data from an AI hiring platform that builds a separate predictive model for each client firm to score low-skill job applicants on likely retention, the authors score every application against every firm's model — not just the model of the firm it was actually submitted to — to build a full firm-by-applicant matrix of predicted outcomes. They find each firm's model predicts well for its own applicants but is no better than chance for applicants who went to other firms, and that an applicant's predicted retention if reassigned to a different firm varies widely and systematically with the applicant's traits. The paper argues that this "model comparison externality" — a platform holding many firm-specific models can reveal how firms genuinely disagree about which traits matter, even within a single low-skill labor market — has value for firms deciding where to invest, for policymakers targeting interventions, and for platforms designing their business models.

## 4. AI-Assisted Classification under Correlation Neglect and Trust [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Dingwen Kong, Liang Lyu, Saurabh Amin, Amine Bennouna, Daniel Huttenlocher, Asuman Ozdaglar
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-04
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05870
**Summary:** This paper models a worker who combines her own signal with an AI recommendation to make a binary decision, driven by two primitives: her awareness of how much her evidence overlaps with the AI's, and how much she trusts the AI's recommendation. The resulting closed-form map over AI capability and evidence overlap shows that a worker who neglects the overlap between her own and the AI's evidence can become less accurate as the AI gets more capable, by double-counting shared information, while miscalibrated trust can either worsen or offset this cost. Testing the model on ChatBench, the authors find AI assistance raises human accuracy by 21 points on average even though workers apply only about a quarter of the statistically optimal weight to AI advice, that GPT-4o-assisted workers fall into a region where full automation would outperform human-AI collaboration while Llama-3.1-8b-assisted workers sit right at the boundary of complementarity, and that correcting trust calibration recovers meaningful accuracy with the more capable model but barely helps with the weaker one.

## 5. Cold-Start Pricing of LLM Services under Two-Sided Learning [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Zhicheng Du, Pinyan Lu, Keyu Wang, Zihe Wang, Xiang Yan, Chunxue Yang
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-02
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03199
**Summary:** This paper models the problem facing a new LLM service provider that must set prices before it has data on costs or demand, while users' own willingness to pay shifts as they gain experience with the service, formalizing this as a two-sided learning game between provider and users. The authors derive matching upper and lower bounds on the provider's regret and show the problem has two regimes: when serving costs are low, a "Calibration-and-Tracking" pricing algorithm achieves the best possible learning rate, while at higher serving costs the provider must also learn whether continuing to serve is profitable at all, which provably makes pricing fundamentally harder. The result implies that how costly it is to serve an LLM product shapes not just a provider's profit margin but the underlying difficulty of learning to price it correctly, a point the authors confirm in simulations against standard online-pricing benchmarks.
