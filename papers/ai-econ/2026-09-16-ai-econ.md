# AI Economics Papers — 2026-09-16

## 1. AI Coding Tools and Digital Entrepreneurship: The Role of Software Expertise [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Ruiqing Cao, Abhishek Bhatia
**Date:** first posted 2025-11-09; revised 2026-09-08
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.06545
**Summary:** Cao and Bhatia study how the diffusion of AI coding tools has changed digital entrepreneurship, measuring each product category's pre-LLM exposure to AI coding and linking it to venture launch, web-traffic, and financing records. They find that exposure raises the rate of new venture launches after AI coding tools spread widely from late 2022, but these AI-exposed entrants are less likely to survive their first year, even as survivors raise more financing. Founders with prior software work experience capture most of the extra launches, cushion the survival decline when development is only partly automated, and account for essentially all of the financing gains, suggesting AI tools complement rather than substitute for technical expertise in digital entrepreneurship.

## 2. Generative AI for Analysts [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Jian Xue, Qian Zhang, Wu Zhu
**Date:** first posted 2025-12-12; revised 2026-09-09
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.19705
**Summary:** Xue, Zhang and Zhu exploit FactSet's 2023 integration of generative AI as a natural experiment to test how AI changes what financial analysts produce, comparing FactSet-linked reports to those from other platforms with placebo checks to rule out a platform-wide trend. They find AI access makes analyst reports richer — more distinct sources, broader topic coverage, more analytical methods, and faster turnaround — but forecast accuracy actually falls when analysts face heavier information-processing demands. Because a machine-learning benchmark fed the same inputs shows no such decline, the authors conclude the bottleneck has shifted from data access to human attention, meaning generative AI raises the volume of information analysts can gather while making their capacity to process it the new binding constraint.

## 3. Mapping AI Economic Complexity [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Daeun Moon, Yeokyung Hwang, Junseok Hwang, Dawoon Jeong
**Date:** 2026-09-15
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17200
**Summary:** Moon, Hwang, Hwang and Jeong adapt the economic-complexity framework, originally built for green technology, to trace countries' productive capabilities in AI-enabling goods, using BACI export data on 103 such products from 2007 to 2023. They construct three measures — a complexity-weighted specialization index, a product-level index of nearby diversification opportunities, and the average complexity of a country's remaining adjacent options — and find Japan leads on current specialization while China leads on future opportunity, with much of the variation explained simply by how broad a country's existing export portfolio is. Early diversification potential predicts subsequent growth in a country's AI-goods export share but not significant gains in complexity itself, giving trade-based evidence on which countries are positioned to move up the AI hardware and components value chain.

## 4. Redistributive Policies for the Times of Transformative AI [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Jakub Growiec, Klaus Prettner, Maciej Szkróbka
**Date:** 2026-09-13
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.14750
**Summary:** Growiec, Prettner and Szkróbka survey the redistributive policy toolkit proposed for an era of transformative AI, working from a common theoretical framework to compare universal basic income, universal basic capital, state-issued permits for compute and robot use, and taxes on capital, compute, robots, tokens, land, energy, consumption and wealth. Their starting point is that broad automation could raise growth while concentrating most of the gains in a narrow set of firms and individuals, pushing inequality beyond levels seen in the industrial era unless offset by policy. They argue that policies distributing the rents from capital broadly — particularly universal basic capital or a capital-tax-financed UBI — are best suited to durably contain the inequality that transformative AI could otherwise generate.

## 5. Access to Live AI Advice and Behavior Under Risk: An Incentivized Experiment [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Paul Althaus, Leon Houf, Christiane Schwieren
**Date:** 2026-09-07
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.07358
**Summary:** Althaus, Houf and Schwieren run an incentivized lottery-choice experiment (N=158) to test whether access to a live, interactive generative-AI advisor changes how people behave under risk, comparing it against a conventional pre-written decision aid and a non-interactive one-shot AI response while holding the information format constant. Using the DOSE procedure to elicit risk preferences, they find no evidence that access to a live, queryable AI advisor shifts measured risk aversion relative to the static alternatives. The null result speaks to the growing literature on AI as an everyday financial and decision advisor, suggesting that interactivity per se may not be what drives any behavioral effects of AI advice on risk-taking.
