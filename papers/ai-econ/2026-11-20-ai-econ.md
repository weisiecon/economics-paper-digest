# AI Economics Papers — 2026-11-20

## 1. The Economics of AI Foundation Models: Openness, Competition, and Governance [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Fasheng Xu, Xiaoyu Wang, Wei Chen, Karen Xie
**Date:** first posted 2025-10-17; revised 2026-11-17
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15200
**Summary:** This theoretical paper models a foundation-model incumbent that sets a license fee and openness level while a deployer adapts the model and builds model-specific experience that creates a "data flywheel" favoring continued use of the incumbent over an entrant. It finds that optimal openness falls and then rises as learning effects strengthen, with the incumbent moving from full openness and high fees to restricted openness for retention and then to low fees that buy enough usage to tolerate greater openness, and shows that policies like openness mandates, vertical integration, or usage subsidies can raise or lower welfare depending on the strength of learning. The results connect AI developers' pricing and openness choices to policy levers, showing that well-intentioned interventions such as subsidies can backfire by inducing fee increases and reduced adaptation.

## 2. Bridging Distant Ideas: the Impact of AI on R&D and Recombinant Innovation [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Emanuele Bazzichi, Massimo Riccaboni, Fulvio Castellacci
**Date:** first posted 2026-04-02; revised 2026-11-13
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02189
**Summary:** The authors embed recombinant innovation in a Schumpeterian quality-ladder growth model where R&D tasks can be automated by AI, which lowers the cost of combining distant knowledge but also speeds up creative destruction and shortens the monopoly rents that reward radical innovation. They find the effect of automating more R&D tasks is non-monotonic: firms initially chase more radical, distant recombinations, but beyond a threshold shift back toward incremental innovation, with aggregate knowledge growth that can rise or become hump-shaped. They further show that the innovation distance firms choose in equilibrium can be socially sub-optimal, giving a welfare rationale for policies that nudge AI-assisted R&D toward more (or less) radical recombination than firms choose on their own.

## 3. AI, Specialization, and the Organization of Knowledge Work: Evidence from Legal Services [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Liu Zhang, Mark Esposito, Isabel Yang
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-17
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23354
**Summary:** This paper builds a framework linking individual self-provision, firms' make-or-buy decisions, and pricing by specialist suppliers to study how cheaper AI-assisted expertise reshapes professional-service markets, applying it to U.S. legal services. A difference-in-differences design across 89 federal districts finds a 6.6-percentage-point rise in non-prisoner self-representation relative to prisoner petitions by fiscal 2025, while lawyer employment shows no comparable shift toward client firms, and the largest law firms grew revenue 13% in 2025 on nearly flat billable hours even as lower-priced firms saw stronger demand growth. The results show that cheaper AI-assisted expertise need not produce either widespread internalization of legal work or falling prices for premium firms, because clients reallocate work across outside providers and premium suppliers can raise markups as price-sensitive work departs.

## 4. Platform Recommendation and Competition in Search Markets [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Sanxi Li, Jun Yu, Mingsheng Zhang
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-18
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24464
**Summary:** The authors develop a framework for evaluating how a platform's recommendation rule shapes seller pricing and advertising incentives and the alternatives consumers expect to find, establishing equilibrium-existence conditions for a broad class of recommendation scores and working through three applications. They show that rewarding low prices more strongly does not necessarily lower equilibrium prices or benefit consumers when product quality is sufficiently differentiated, that quality-based guidance can lower prices while raising average transaction prices, and that no recommendation rule using positive weights on both quality and low prices is ever optimal, even when the platform cares about consumer surplus. The findings imply that seemingly consumer-friendly recommendation designs on AI-mediated search and shopping platforms can have counterintuitive, even welfare-reducing, effects on market outcomes.

## 5. Paying Creators for Training Data Can Increase AI Displacement [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Lijuan Luo, Emaad Manzoor, Nathan Yang, Fan Yao
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-15
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20204
**Summary:** The authors model a platform where creators decide whether to supply training data and consumers choose between buying from creators and using generative AI trained on that data, comparing regimes with and without mandated compensation for training-data use. They find that under compensation, low-quality creators become pure data suppliers while AI fully displaces their consumer demand even though AI quality and pricing are unchanged, whereas without compensation the platform must protect creator demand to keep their data, which also caps further AI displacement as AI improves. Compensation raises creator payoffs and total surplus but reduces consumer surplus, showing that policies meant to protect creators from AI can paradoxically accelerate their displacement from the market they used to serve.
