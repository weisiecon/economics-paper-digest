# AI Economics Papers — 2026-11-09

## 1. Does Frontier AI Strengthen Cyber Defense? Evidence from Project Glasswing [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Thomas Mosk
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-04
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06629
**Summary:** This paper treats Project Glasswing's early release of a Claude vulnerability-finding model to a small group of large US tech firms as a natural experiment, using the universe of publicly disclosed software vulnerabilities to measure how frontier-AI access changes defenders', researchers', and attackers' behavior. Treated firms more than doubled their vulnerability disclosures and raised high-impact disclosures by about 150% relative to Chinese and Russian firms without access, external bug-bounty researchers shifted their reporting toward untreated firms without exiting the market, and exploitation rates for disclosed flaws fell sharply. The author concludes that frontier AI access strengthened cyber defense for firms that got it, but its restricted release created a cross-country asymmetry in software security.

## 2. Artificial Shoppers and Attention Competition [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Xiaomeng Guo, Fasheng Xu, Xiaoli Zhang
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-06
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09399
**Summary:** This theoretical paper models a platform that hosts two of three differentiated-quality products and compares price competition, platform profit, and consumer welfare across three regimes: no AI (consumers compare products under salience-driven attribute distortion), AI assistance (an assistant recommends the two best products but consumers still compare them under the same distortion), and AI delegation (an agent picks the purchase subject to approval). It shows assistance can soften price competition even though it expands product access, and identifies an "alignment paradox" in which some consumers end up worse off than without AI despite the assistant always recommending their true-best option, because salience can still steer them toward a cheaper but lower-quality pick. Which AI design the platform prefers, and whether that design raises or lowers aggregate consumer surplus relative to no AI, depends on which pair of products is hosted, so platform-optimal AI assistance is not uniformly welfare-improving.

## 3. Reducing SNAP Payment Errors at Lower Cost With AI: A Simulation Study [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Alex Chohlas-Wood
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-02
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03213
**Summary:** Using public SNAP quality-control data, this paper simulates how US states could target limited staff capacity for preauthorization review toward benefit applications most likely to contain payment errors, comparing a statistical risk-scoring approach against a newer approach in which AI flags specific discrepancies for reviewers to check. It finds that combining AI flags with risk scores achieves a given reduction in the payment error rate at roughly one-seventh the cost of risk-based review alone, and that this cost advantage holds across a wide range of plausible assumptions. The result is economically relevant because new federal legislation ties states' cost-sharing obligations to their payment error rates, making AI-assisted review a potentially large cost saver for state budgets.
