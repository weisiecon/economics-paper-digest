# AI Economics Papers — 2026-09-23

## 1. AI and the Market for Signals [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Doruk Cetemen, Emre Ozdenoren
**Date:** first posted 2026-09-15
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17752
**Summary:** Cetemen and Ozdenoren extend a career-signaling model by adding an AI industry that sells "tokens" workers can pass off as their own output, comparing regimes with no trading, one-way purchases, and open two-way trade. They show that token purchases by low-ability workers raise the bar high-ability workers must clear to separate themselves, so the effect on effort flips with the trading regime: buyers work less (even below the full-information benchmark), while under open trade with abundant AI the signal stops responding to effort at all and every type under-invests in skill. Whether AI leaves workers better or worse off hinges on whether their output has independent value and on the trading regime, since interventions like token taxes, wage taxes, or watermarking have ambiguous, regime-dependent effects on welfare.

## 2. Why a Non-Discriminatory Royalty Surcharge Is Not Chip-Neutral: The Error in FTC v. Qualcomm [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Sang-Seung Yi
**Date:** first posted 2026-09-16
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18161
**Summary:** Yi builds an equilibrium model of the modem-chip market to test the Ninth Circuit's claim, in FTC v. Qualcomm, that Qualcomm's per-handset royalty surcharge was "chip neutral" because it did not vary with which chip a phone used. The model shows the surcharge behaves differently from an equivalent government tax: because Qualcomm keeps the surcharge even on handsets using a rival's chip, raising its own price costs Qualcomm less than it costs a rival, so the surcharge tilts handset makers toward Qualcomm rather than leaving their choices undistorted. This undercuts the legal defense's economic premise and extends to ad valorem and discriminatory variants, with direct implications for how antitrust courts should evaluate loyalty-inducing licensing terms in chip markets.

## 3. Welfare-Opaque Income: Taxation under AI-Agent Delegation [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Yukun Zhang, Kemu Xu, Yishen Chen
**Date:** first posted 2026-09-17
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20425
**Summary:** Zhang, Xu and Chen study optimal income taxation when taxpayers delegate economic decisions to an AI agent whose internal decision rule the government cannot observe, creating a "double unobservability" in which identical tax-base responses can conceal different welfare consequences. They derive an optimal-tax condition that augments the usual sufficient statistics with an "execution wedge," then run a controlled lab test of 4,500 model runs across five AI engines and find the engines diverge sharply in how faithfully they execute a taxpayer's stated objective versus a conflicting one. The results imply that as AI agents mediate more economic choices, tax authorities need information about how those agents execute delegated decisions, not just about observed income, to correctly infer the welfare effects of reform.

## 4. Mitigating Emergent Collusion in LLM Pricing Agents [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Abdullah Garra
**Date:** first posted 2026-09-11
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13037
**Summary:** Garra replicates prior findings that LLM-based pricing agents can settle into supracompetitive, collusion-like prices in repeated oligopoly simulations even without being instructed to coordinate, reproducing the effect with DeepSeek-V3.1 (though less extreme than earlier GPT-4 results). The paper tests three regulatory interventions — a prompt-based warning, a Harrington-style expected-damages penalty, and an active random new entrant — finding the prompt warning only partially works, the damages-based regulator pushes prices close to the competitive Nash benchmark, and adding a real entrant has the strongest effect of all. The findings suggest that curbing algorithmic collusion among AI pricing agents requires regulation that changes market structure or payoffs directly, rather than instructions asking the agents to behave competitively.

## 5. The network advantage: benefits of interregional scientific knowledge spillovers on AI patenting [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Saverio Barabuffi, Jacopo Cricchio, Alberto Di Minin, Guido Pialli
**Date:** first posted 2026-09-22
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26604
**Summary:** Using a network-econometric approach across 26 Chinese provincial regions from 2006–2020, the authors test whether interregional spillovers from co-authored AI research, rather than the more commonly studied co-invention ties, raise a region's AI patenting output. They find these scientific-layer spillovers do increase AI patenting, but the gains are captured almost entirely by regions where private enterprises dominate R&D, while more state-driven regions fail to convert the same knowledge flows into patents. The result points to ownership structure, not just network position, as a key determinant of which regions turn interregional AI knowledge diffusion into innovation output, with implications for regional AI-industrial policy.
