# AI Economics Papers — 2026-11-02

## 1. Renegotiable Options Fail to Prevent Hold-Up Between Two Profit-Maximizing Frontier LLM Agents [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Junwei Yao, Zike Yang, Yongxi Zhou, Yanxian Chen, Jianan Liu, Jiaxi Wen, Zhijun Wang
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-29
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40961
**Summary:** The authors replicate a classic laboratory contract-theory experiment (Hoppe and Schmitz) by having Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol play profit-maximizing buyer and seller roles in a one-shot hold-up game under four contract regimes. Unlike the human subjects, for whom a renegotiable buyer option was shown to raise investment, the AI agents followed textbook game-theoretic logic exactly: no seller invested under the renegotiable option that theory predicts should fail, and every seller invested once renegotiation was blocked. The result shows that a contract term empirically proven to raise investment between humans does not transfer to profit-maximizing LLM agents, with implications for designing contracts in markets where AI agents negotiate on firms' behalf.

## 2. Screening AI-equipped applicants [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Pengyu Qian, Sophie H. Yu
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-25
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34508
**Summary:** The paper models an employer who must screen job candidates whose generative-AI-produced application materials can be manipulated at a privately known cost, and characterizes the welfare-optimal hiring rule under a capacity constraint. As manipulation ability becomes more dispersed among equally-qualified applicants, the authors show the optimal policy converges to screening on observable quality alone and ignoring applicants' reports entirely, a result they call "strategic feature collapse." They then design a simple dual-track mechanism, admitting some applicants directly on observable quality and offering others a report-based route, that recovers most of the welfare richer screening could capture despite the problem's multidimensional complexity.

## 3. The Capability Transfer: Cohort Reversal, Codified Expertise, and the Adaptability Tax of AI Transformation [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Ina Catrinescu
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-22
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33102
**Summary:** Extending an existing two-period model of AI and labor to a firm with junior and senior cohorts, the author studies what happens to the pipeline that trains new experts once AI automates the most codified, junior-level work first. In the calibrated baseline, junior labor demand falls roughly a fifth before partially recovering, but the stock of experienced seniors declines more and keeps falling as retirees are not replaced, while the time needed to develop a new expert through unassisted practice rises from about five to nine years. The paper highlights a mismatch in incentives: a manager optimizing near-term output over a short horizon cuts wage costs substantially while depleting the firm's future capacity on tasks AI cannot do, and proposes three measurable indicators firms could track to detect this erosion before it shows up in output.

## 4. Is the education gap in generative AI use smaller than the internet's was at a similar level of use? Evidence from 16 European countries [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Keisuke Watanabe
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-26
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36181
**Summary:** Using Eurostat ICT-usage survey data from 35 European countries, the author asks whether the 2025 gap in generative-AI adoption between highly and less educated adults is narrower than the equivalent education gap in internet adoption was, measured at a comparable overall level of technology use in each country's own history. Matching 16 countries to their comparable "internet year," the paper finds the generative-AI education gap is smaller in the large majority of matched countries, a result that survives most robustness checks restricted to specific age groups, though its size is sensitive to how education composition is handled and a log-ratio version of the result is more fragile. The author flags that the analysis was not pre-registered and that the findings describe unadjusted cross-country contrasts in a non-random sample rather than a causal effect or an EU-wide generalization.

## 5. The uneven rise of AI-generated web content [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Adam Visokay, Soumyakanti Pan, Molly Griston, Polina Iasakova, Mark Nepf, Denis Peskoff, Riya Sinha, Jikai Sun, Isabelle Thiara, Letao Wang, Yiwei Wu, Lauren Yan, Tyler H. McCormick
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-27
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36631
**Summary:** Scanning more than five million crawlable webpages with multiple AI-text detectors and correcting for detector error using a Bayesian hierarchical model, the authors track how the share of AI-generated text on the public web has grown since generative AI's emergence. They find the increase is highly uneven: English-language pages show more AI-generated text than pages in other languages, .gov and .edu domains show much less than .com, and growth has been fastest on the least-central, least-linked-to pages of the web while the most central pages remain least likely to carry AI-generated text. The findings matter for understanding how AI is reshaping the economics of information production and the reliability of the public information ecosystem that search engines, AI assistants, and ordinary users draw on.
