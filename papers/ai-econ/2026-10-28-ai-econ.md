# AI Economics Papers — 2026-10-28

## 1. AI Adoption and Employment Expectations: Evidence from a Survey of Small Business Owners [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Will Aarons, Asani Sarkar
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-08
**Source:** https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/10/ai-adoption-and-employment-expectations-evidence-from-a-survey-of-small-business-owners/ (Federal Reserve Bank of New York, Liberty Street Economics)
**Summary:** Using the New York Fed's 2025 Small Business Credit Survey, the authors compare twelve-month employment and revenue expectations between small firms that have adopted AI tools and those that have not, controlling for firm size, sector, owner characteristics, and location. They find AI-using firms are systematically more optimistic about both future hiring and revenue growth than non-adopters, with revenue optimism concentrated among adopters who report operational difficulties implementing the technology. Because the comparison is cross-sectional rather than causally identified, the finding is best read as evidence that AI adoption and firm-level growth expectations move together at the small-business level, a useful complement to firm-survey evidence on how AI diffusion is reshaping expectations even where aggregate employment and pay data show little movement.

## 2. Dynamic Mechanism and Information Design in AI-Agent Environments: A General Analytical Framework [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Guoqiang Tian
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-08; revised 2026-10-10
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11177
**Summary:** The paper builds a general dynamic contracting framework for principals who must design incentives, information disclosure, and monitoring for AI agents whose hidden information and hidden actions evolve over time, nesting dynamic screening, hidden-action contracting, and dynamic information design as special cases. Using a dynamic revelation principle and envelope/integral-monotonicity methods, it decomposes the designer's cost into three distinct margins — dynamic information rents, verification rents, and the implementation cost of hidden action — and shows that more informative monitoring expands what a designer can achieve but that full disclosure is not generally optimal, since information use can itself change an agent's incentive to misbehave. The framework is explicitly motivated by firms contracting with and governing AI agents, but its results on multi-agent settings (correlated information, peer discipline, coupled incentives) extend classical dynamic agency theory to any environment with repeated AI-agent delegation.

## 3. Rethinking Data Value: Asymmetric Data Shapley for Structure-Aware Valuation in AI Model Markets [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Xi Zheng, Yinghui Huang, Xiangyu Chang, Ruoxi Jia, Yong Tan
**Date:** first posted 2025-11-17; revised 2026-10-27
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.12863
**Summary:** The authors argue that standard Shapley-value approaches to pricing contributions in AI data and model markets ignore the fact that many contributions (derivative data, fine-tuning services) are produced from other contributions upstream, so valuing them independently misattributes value toward redundant downstream data and away from the upstream data and services that made it possible. They formalize this as a pipeline-context valuation problem, propose an "Asymmetric Data Shapley" allocation rule that evaluates each contribution only among peers sharing its production context, and give an axiomatic characterization of the resulting allocation together with scalable estimators for large markets. The contribution is closer to market-design theory than to applied economics — it establishes a defensible pricing rule for a structural feature of AI supply chains rather than measuring an actual market outcome — but it speaks directly to how AI data and model markets could be designed to reward upstream contributors rather than only the parties closest to the final product.
