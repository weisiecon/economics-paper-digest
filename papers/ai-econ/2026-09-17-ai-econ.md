# AI Economics Papers — 2026-09-17

## 1. AI and the Market for Signals [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Doruk Cetemen, Emre Ozdenoren
**Date:** 2026-09-15
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17752
**Summary:** Cetemen and Ozdenoren embed an AI industry that sells "tokens" convertible into output indistinguishable from a worker's own into a career-signaling model, then compare no-token-trade, purchase-only, and open-trade-with-side-sales regimes. They show token purchases let low types buy output but raise the bar high types must clear, so high-ability workers can end up working harder while buyers work less than under full information, and under abundant AI with side sales the signal stops reflecting effort at all, causing universal under-investment in skill. Workers gain from AI token trade only when it also raises welfare, and the wedge between the two is exactly the AI industry's profit, while token taxes, wage taxes, and watermarking all have ambiguous welfare effects depending on whether side sales are possible.

## 2. Mechanism Design for Alignment and Control [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Dirk Bergemann, Andrew Koh, Stephen Morris
**Date:** 2026-09-01
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.01595
**Summary:** Bergemann, Koh and Morris develop a mechanism-design framework for principals who must delegate tasks to AI agents whose preferences and true capabilities are unobserved, where capabilities can be hidden but not faked. They derive a revelation principle and a nested-cyclical-monotonicity characterization of which delegation policies are implementable, and show when eliciting agents' higher-order beliefs disciplines multiple agents at once. The framework yields concrete implications for sandbagging (a capable agent feigning weakness), an alignment-interpretability trade-off, peer-scoring discipline, and reward designs that induce productive competition among AI agents, giving economists a formal toolkit for AI governance problems.

## 3. Why a Non-Discriminatory Royalty Surcharge Is Not Chip-Neutral: The Error in FTC v. Qualcomm [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Sang-Seung Yi
**Date:** 2026-09-16
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18161
**Summary:** Yi builds an equilibrium model of the modem-chip market to test Qualcomm's "chip-neutral" defense of its No License, No Chips policy, which the Ninth Circuit accepted in reversing the district court's antitrust ruling. He shows that a flat per-handset royalty surcharge is not equivalent to an equal government tax: because Qualcomm keeps the surcharge collected even on rivals' chips while a tax is remitted to the Treasury, the surcharge distorts Qualcomm's pricing incentives less than a true tax would, tilting handset makers toward Qualcomm's chips. The result overturns the surcharge's facial neutrality argument for both per-handset and ad valorem royalty structures, giving competition regulators a structural economic rebuttal to the appellate court's reasoning.

## 4. Sophistication in GenAI Use: Field Evidence from a Large Firm [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Nicholas J. Hallman, Zachary T. Kowaleski, Anu Puvvada, Jaime J. Schmidt
**Date:** 2026-08-27
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27364
**Summary:** Using proprietary logs of 713,564 employee prompts and LLM responses from nearly 4,000 back-office workers across 15 functions over eight months in 2025, the authors measure how "sophisticated" genAI use varies within a single large firm. They find senior employees use genAI more sophisticatedly, consistent with domain expertise complementing AI capability, and that sophistication peaks in Strategy, Digital Innovation, and Project Management functions focused on firmwide change. Sophistication neither improves with tenure on the job nor persists after formal AI training, suggesting firms face a real and durable constraint on how well workers exploit genAI tools, with direct implications for how fast AI adoption actually raises firm productivity.

## 5. Mitigating Emergent Collusion in LLM Pricing Agents [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Abdullah Garra
**Date:** 2026-09-11
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13037
**Summary:** Garra replicates the finding that LLM-based pricing agents can reach supracompetitive prices in repeated oligopoly without explicit collusion instructions, reproducing the prompt-sensitivity effect from prior GPT-4 work using DeepSeek-V3.1. He then tests three regulatory interventions: a prompt-only warning, a Harrington-style expected-damages payoff regulator, and an active random entrant, finding that only interventions changing agents' actual incentives or market structure meaningfully curb above-Nash pricing, while warning prompts alone leave most of the effect intact. The active-entrant intervention is the most effective, pushing prices below the relevant Nash benchmark, suggesting competition-policy tools aimed at market structure will outperform disclosure-based AI regulation for algorithmic pricing.
