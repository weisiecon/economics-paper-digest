# AI Economics Papers — 2026-11-17

## 1. When AI Gets Cheaper, Do Prices Follow? Information Delays and Algorithmic Pricing [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Krish Tadigotla
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-20
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31693
**Summary:** This paper asks whether falling AI inference costs actually translate into lower consumer prices by simulating a duopoly of Q-learning sellers with logit demand across 960 training runs that vary how quickly rivals observe each other's prices, how often prices can be revised, and whether costs fall. It finds that even under the shortest information delay and fastest repricing, firms pass through only 44-56% of a cost reduction to consumers, and that learned pricing policies leave consumer surplus below the static Nash benchmark in every condition tested. The results suggest cost declines in AI provision need not flow through to buyers, independent of any explicit collusion, which matters for how much of the AI productivity dividend reaches consumers versus providers.

## 2. When Better Artificial Intelligence Widens the Adoption Gap in Health Care [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Jonas Fooken, Ali Furkan Kalay, Metin Uyanik
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-16
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20561
**Summary:** This theoretical paper models how healthcare providers' influence over two policy levers, how much care is shifted to AI and how many paid human reviews must still accompany it, shapes AI adoption as the technology becomes more capable. It shows that providers' gains from adoption first rise then fall as AI improves, so providers favor assistive AI that preserves their review income but oppose sufficiently autonomous AI that displaces it, and when providers can sway adoption but not the review requirement, their opposition produces under-adoption that grows worse as AI capability increases. The result identifies provider incentives, rather than AI capability itself, as a key driver of why healthcare adoption of AI can lag its potential patient benefit.

## 3. Strategic Competition and Convergence in LLM Leaderboards [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Naveen Raman, Fei Fang, Yiling Chen
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-06
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09780
**Summary:** This paper models leaderboard competition among LLM developers as a game in which firms allocate resources across capability dimensions to maximize win rate, then tests the model's predictions against data from LM Arena and OpenRouter. It finds that similar production functions push developers toward "monoculture" in capability profiles while comparative advantage lets firms sustain distinct strengths, and empirically developers improve fastest in categories where they lag rivals even as the competitive frontier becomes more crowded and usage patterns diversify. The findings suggest that intensifying competition among AI firms need not erode product differentiation, with implications for how benchmark-driven competition shapes market structure in the AI industry.

## 4. Cold-Start Pricing of LLM Services under Two-Sided Learning [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Zhicheng Du, Pinyan Lu, Keyu Wang, Zihe Wang, Xiang Yan, Chunxue Yang
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-02
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03199
**Summary:** This paper studies how an LLM provider should price its service when it lacks historical demand data and users' willingness to pay itself evolves with usage, formalizing the problem as two-sided learning between provider and users. It derives matching upper and lower regret bounds showing a phase transition: at low serving costs an algorithm achieving the optimal $\Theta(T^{1/3})$ regret suffices, but at higher costs the provider must also learn whether continued service is profitable at all, which raises the optimal regret to $\Theta(\sqrt{T})$. The result shows that serving costs do not just set profit margins for AI service providers but fundamentally change how hard it is to learn a profitable price path during a market's early, data-scarce phase.

## 5. Accuracy Is Not Welfare: Evaluating AI Buyers under Strategic Price Complexity in a Synthetic Duopoly [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Ning Jiaqu
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-12
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16471
**Summary:** This paper evaluates AI shopping-assistant pipelines in a synthetic duopoly where sellers can obscure true prices, comparing a calculator-style assistant against one that relies on sellers' verified-total disclosures. It finds that the calculator-based assistant picks the objectively best offer far more often (84% vs. 69% of the time), yet the disclosure-based assistant delivers meaningfully higher worst-case consumer surplus in equilibrium, and this ranking can flip depending on whether sellers are assumed to hold fixed strategies or to re-optimize against the assistant's behavior. The result argues that accuracy metrics for AI buying agents are a poor proxy for consumer welfare once seller incentives to exploit price complexity are taken into account.
