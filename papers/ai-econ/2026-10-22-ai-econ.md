# AI Economics Papers — 2026-10-22

## 1. Is Anyone Enforcing the Cartel? What Reinforcement Learning Teaches LLM Agents in Markets [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Hieu Tran, Hong Yu
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-20
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28787
**Summary:** The authors argue that prior claims of LLM pricing agents "colluding" conflate correlated outcomes with an enforceable cartel, since a cartel only holds if a member who cheats on the agreed price is punished; they introduce an audit that forces a member to cheat in counterfactual copies of a market to test whether agents actually learn and apply deterrence. Testing reinforcement-learning and prompted LLM agents, they find that agents mostly learn fixed rules from self-play rather than genuine punishment strategies, and that only certain Q-learning setups enforce collusion through matching rival price cuts, and then only weakly and against large deviations. The results show that outcome-based measures of algorithmic collusion can be misleading, with direct implications for how antitrust regulators should assess AI pricing agents.

## 2. Automation, Savings, and Breakaway to a Post-Labour Economy [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Omer Edhan, Ziv Hellman
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-05
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06296
**Summary:** The authors formalize "automation breakaway" as the condition under which saving generated by physical capital and AI-driven "artificial labour" becomes self-sustaining enough to finance ever more automation, modeling artificial labour as production-like but capital-like in how it accumulates. They show productivity growth and the composition of factor income are distinct channels supporting breakaway, and that near full automation, aggregate human labour income can shrink even as average income in surviving human jobs stays positive or rises. The model offers a tractable way to characterize when and how an economy tips into a self-reinforcing automation dynamic, with direct implications for how growth and labor-income shares evolve under rapid AI-driven automation.

## 3. Inference Auctions [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Keegan Harris, Siddharth Prasad, Asher Trockman, Nika Haghtalab, Michael I. Jordan
**Date:** first posted 2026-09-30
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.40070
**Summary:** The authors design an auction mechanism for allocating scarce inference compute among LLM API users with heterogeneous latency tolerance, replacing today's coarse fixed-price service tiers with bids for faster service, and derive fast algorithms that implement prices incentivizing truthful bidding. They also build an autobidding agent that lets a user specify a total inference budget and dynamically adjusts bids over time to maximize utility subject to that budget. Experiments show the mechanism raises overall system welfare relative to existing priority schemes while preserving the latency and cache-efficiency advantages of state-of-the-art inference serving systems, offering a concrete market design for the emerging compute/inference market.

## 4. Price of Anarchy of First-Price Auctions in Agentic Markets [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Shubhendu Trivedi, Jacob Eisenstein, Alekh Agarwal, Ian Gemp, Michael Kaisers, Adam Fisch
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-14
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20587
**Summary:** The authors study the welfare loss from using first-price auctions to allocate user prompts among competing AI agents in a decentralized "agentic market," since first-price auctions are not truthful and can misallocate prompts away from the bidder who values them most. They show that two features likely to hold in practice — a "competitive floor" where several bidders value a prompt near the optimal welfare (e.g., because strong models get distilled into similar cheaper ones), and near-independence of agents' values once the prompt's difficulty is fixed — sharply reduce this welfare loss relative to the standard worst-case bound, and validate the theory empirically with simulated strategic bidders on a real prompt-routing dataset. The results give an efficiency argument for simple, decentralized AI agent markets instead of more complex truthful mechanisms.

## 5. The Safe Harbor Paradox: When Combining Human and AI Skills Creates or Destroys Value [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Riccardo Zanardelli
**Date:** first posted 2025-09-17; revised 2026-09-28
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.14057
**Summary:** Zanardelli models human oversight of AI as a high-variance economic bet rather than a safeguard, showing that combining human and AI skills creates economic value only when genuine augmentation is achieved and destroys value otherwise, even when AI outperforms humans on average and even in high-error-cost settings. Using Monte Carlo simulation, the paper decomposes augmentation into a routing channel (assigning each task to the better skill) and a synergy channel (gains from a well-designed workflow), and maps the conditions under which oversight succeeds or backfires. The findings suggest blanket human-oversight mandates can create false security, and that firms adopting human-AI workflows need deliberate design rather than assuming oversight alone adds value.
