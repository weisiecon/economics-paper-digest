# AI Economics Papers — 2026-11-22

## 1. Practice Makes Perfect: Tacit Knowledge, Generative AI, and Returns to Schooling and Experience [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Eduardo Levy Yeyati, Darío Judzik, Juan Ignacio Barbiero
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-26
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36008
**Summary:** The authors argue that generative AI makes codified knowledge cheap to access at the point of production while practice-based judgment stays tied to the worker, which should lower the wage return to schooling and raise the return to early-career experience in occupations most exposed to the technology. Using US Current Population Survey earnings data from 2018 through mid-2026 and the pre-ChatGPT Felten-Raj-Seamans AI exposure index, they find the schooling return falls by 0.55 log points and the experience return rises at low tenure but falls at high tenure in exposed occupations after ChatGPT's release, with the effects surviving a pandemic placebo and alternative sample restrictions. The results point to a broad repricing of human capital, in which codifiable schooling-based knowledge becomes less scarce while the value of experience depends on whether it transfers to the new technology.

## 2. Signaling in the Age of AI: Evidence from Cover Letters [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Jingyi Cui, Gabriel Dias, Justin Ye
**Date:** first posted 2025-09-29; revised 2026-11-10
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25054
**Summary:** Using the rollout of an AI-powered cover-letter tool on a large online labor platform, with data on both access and usage at the application level, the authors run a difference-in-differences design to see how generative AI changes labor-market signaling. They find that access to the tool raised the textual alignment between cover letters and job posts and increased callback rates, but the correlation between that alignment and callbacks fell by roughly half after the tool's introduction, consistent with AI eroding the signal value of a cover letter. Employers responded by leaning more on other signals such as applicants' prior work histories, showing how a widely available AI writing tool can degrade one market signal and push employers toward others.

## 3. Are Revealed Preferences All You Need for AI Alignment? [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Joshua S. Gans, Richard T. Holden
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-14
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19417
**Summary:** The authors model AI alignment as a principal-agent problem in which an AI agent must infer what its principal wants from the actions the principal takes, and ask when those revealed choices alone give the AI everything it needs. They show that for choosing actions, observed decisions are sufficient, but when the AI must also choose which costless evidence to gather, decisions reveal enough only when the same evidence would be best under every belief consistent with the principal's choices; otherwise the AI can act as the principal would yet still gather the wrong evidence, and greater AI capability can turn that evidential error into a worse action. The result gives a formal account of why training AI purely on revealed behavior can be insufficient for alignment, and ties the risk to how coarsely a principal's actions partition its underlying beliefs.

## Excluded, borderline

- **From the firm- to the application-level: A new typology of AI use in organisations** (Castellacci, arXiv:2611.15371) — a conceptual taxonomy of firm-level AI applications illustrated with documented use cases, but no causal identification or structural model, so it falls short of the research-design bar.
- **Same Text, Different Filename: Are LLM Judges Biased?** (Popov, arXiv:2611.01829) — a well-executed audit of filename/label bias in LLM manuscript screening, but it produces no market, firm, or aggregate-outcome finding beyond the bias itself.
- **Could It Have Fit Anything? Restrictiveness of Economic Models, LLMs, and AI Workflows** (Fudenberg, Gao & You, arXiv:2602.07688) — a methodological comparison of how restrictive economic models are versus AI prediction procedures; about evaluating AI-as-a-tool for economic modeling, not AI's market or aggregate economic effects.
- **Can Artificial Intelligence Forecast Treatment Effects in Economics?** (Vivalt, arXiv:2610.38883) — evaluates LLMs as a research-forecasting tool against the AEA RCT Registry; AI-for-economics-research methodology rather than the economics of AI.
