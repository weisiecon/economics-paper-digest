# AI Economics Papers — 2026-10-23

## 1. Joules-to-GDP: How Much Economic Value Do AI Systems Produce per Unit of Energy? [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Amirreza Zeinali, Jon Saad-Falcon, Avanika Narayan, Mersad Abbasi, Jonathan S. Hartley, John Hennessy, Christopher Re, Azalia Mirhoseini
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-22
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30858
**Summary:** The authors introduce two energy-adjusted measures of AI's economic payoff — net dollar value and expert time saved per joule consumed — and compute them across 220 real-world tasks spanning 44 occupations, running five open-weight models on four different AI accelerator types. They find that the occupations and models producing the most dollar value or succeeding most often are not necessarily the most energy-efficient: software development returns substantially more value per joule than engineering despite generating less total dollar value per task, and a smaller model can deliver most of a leading model's economic value using a fraction of its energy. The framework gives a concrete, measurable basis for assessing how much aggregate economic value AI systems create relative to the energy they consume, bearing directly on debates over AI's macroeconomic and environmental footprint.

## 2. Decentralized LLM Workflows via Sequential Auctions [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Ian Gemp, Shubhendu Trivedi, Adam Fisch, Fantine Huot, Michael Kaisers, Mirella Lapata, William W. Cohen, Alekh Agarwal, Kate Larson, Jacob Eisenstein
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-15
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22201
**Summary:** The authors study how a decentralized market of self-interested AI agents can solve multi-step tasks through sequential auctions, showing both theoretically and empirically that the two textbook extremes — perfectly cloned agents, which drives profit to zero, and markets with no real competition — each cause the market to fail. Testing four bidding algorithms across three auction formats (first-price, second-price, and credit-conserving Vickrey) in diverse task domains, they find that genuinely heterogeneous agents operating under imperfect competition learn to shade their bids just enough to sustain positive profit margins while still completing collaborative tasks successfully and cheaply. The results offer a concrete market-design lesson for the emerging economy of AI agents: sustaining such markets requires imperfect, not perfect, competition.

## 3. Paying for Information, Not for Clicks: A Block-Level Publisher Content Marketplace for AI Answers [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Markus Mobius, Luca Braghieri, Sarah Eichmeyer, Daniel G. Goldstein, Nicole Immorlica, Oliver Kiss, Ro'ee Levy, Brendan Lucier, Ishan Mahajan, Jingyi Qiu, Tanya Rosenblat, David M. Rothschild, Andras Sari, Alexsandrs Slivkins, Colin Watson
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-21
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30324
**Summary:** The authors design a marketplace for compensating news publishers when their reporting underlies AI-generated search answers, introducing a "lego block" unit of attribution — a single fact or claim that may appear across multiple publishers' articles — and deriving three Shapley-value-based payment rules: equal sharing, rewarding whoever reported a fact first, and rewarding indispensable contributions. Testing the rules on real answers to nearly 2,000 search queries about a heavily covered news story, they find the rules nearly agree when attribution only considers the handful of sources retrieved for one query, but diverge sharply once a global index reveals the true overlap in publishers' reporting, shifting several percentage points of value toward or away from premium outlets depending on which rule is chosen. The findings show that a workable AI-answer compensation scheme needs a comprehensive, shared record of which publisher reported what and when, with direct relevance to the fast-emerging market for licensing content to AI answer engines.

## 4. The Customer Is the Builder: How AI Coding Agents Are Rewriting the Economics of Software [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Ripon Chandra Malo, Zimeng Yu, Tong Qiu
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-21
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30307
**Summary:** The authors argue that improving AI coding agents are shifting the "make-or-buy" boundary for enterprise software, defining a "software substitution frontier" of functionality organizations can now build and maintain themselves for less than the cost of buying it, and tracking that frontier's growth through benchmark task lengths that leading coding agents can complete, which have been doubling roughly every 129 days since 2023. Using public data, they find mixed market evidence: purchased software has continued to gain U.S. investment share and installations, yet in an organizational survey 32% of respondents reported forgoing at least one software purchase because their own staff could build the needed functionality with coding agents instead. The authors frame their benchmark-based projections as an extrapolation rather than a forecast, but the gap between rising build capacity and still-growing software purchases is itself a warning that a shift in enterprise buying behavior could show up in individual decisions before it becomes visible in aggregate market data.
