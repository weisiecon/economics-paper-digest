# AI Economics Papers — 2026-09-20

## 1. The Economics of Recursive Self-Improvement [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Tom Cunningham, Lukas Althoff, Basil Halperin, Brian Jabarian, Andrew Koh, Arjun Ramani, Phil Trammell, Parker Whitfill, Cheryl Wu
**Date:** 2026-09-14
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15802
**Summary:** The authors build a sequence of directed-graph models of AI R&D feedback loops to formalize when improving AI systems could accelerate their own future improvement, showing that net acceleration depends on the product of elasticities around each loop and hinges on whether gains in "narrow" AI-R&D benchmarks actually transfer to "broad" economically valuable capabilities. They compile existing estimates of the model's key parameters, propose a wish list of data that AI labs could disclose to sharpen those estimates, and calibrate a back-of-the-envelope version of the model. Their central finding is that current feedback loops are not yet strong enough to produce a self-sustaining acceleration in AI capabilities, though the loops appear to be strengthening over time.

## 2. Recommendation Quality and the Concentration of Consumption: Experimental Evidence from Netflix [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Guy Aridor, Winston Chou, Nathan Kallus, Antoine Scheid, Allen Tren, Kevin Zielincki
**Date:** 2026-08-21
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21274
**Summary:** Using a randomized experiment covering 8.5 million Netflix users, the authors test how upgrading the platform's recommender system changes what gets watched, comparing "superstar," "middle-tail," and "long-tail" titles. Better recommendations increase total viewing and make users lean more heavily on algorithmic suggestions, but rather than concentrating consumption further on already-popular titles, the gains flow mainly to a wider set of moderately popular "middle-tail" titles, with little change at the very top or very bottom. The result pushes back on fears that AI-driven recommendation systems homogenize consumption toward superstars, and instead implies that as algorithms and platforms scale, the economic returns to producing solid middle-tier content rise.

## 3. Sophistication in GenAI Use: Field Evidence from a Large Firm [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Nicholas J. Hallman, Zachary T. Kowaleski, Anu Puvvada, Jaime J. Schmidt
**Date:** 2026-08-27
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27364
**Summary:** Drawing on proprietary logs of 713,564 employee prompts to a large language model from nearly 4,000 back-office employees across 15 functions over eight months, the authors construct firm-level measures of how "sophisticated" workers' generative-AI use is. They find senior employees use genAI more sophisticatedly, consistent with domain expertise complementing the tool, and that sophistication is highest in strategy, digital innovation, and project-management roles tied to firmwide change. Sophistication neither improves organically over time nor responds durably to formal AI training, suggesting that within-firm diffusion of effective AI use is slower and stickier than adoption rates alone would suggest.

## 4. Algorithmic Attention and Content Creation on Social Media Platforms [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Yi Chen, Fei Li, Marcel Preuss
**Date:** 2026-09-14
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15573
**Summary:** The authors model an ad-funded social media platform that allocates scarce viewer attention through its recommendation algorithm, trading off ad revenue against the exposure it gives content creators, in a two-sided setting with private information on both sides. The optimal recommendation policy deliberately shows some viewers content that is not their ex post best match, in order to exploit network externalities, and this distortion systematically excludes low-ability creators while cross-subsidizing high-ability ones through extra exposure or payments. Because these platform-side complementarities reshape the variety and quality of content that gets produced, the authors draw out implications for how personalization regulation and advertising-market rules should be designed.

## 5. Whom Do AI Agents Work For? Role Assignment Induces Sponsorship Bias in LLM Recommenders [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Davood Wadi, Yu Ma
**Date:** 2026-09-16
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17989
**Summary:** In controlled choice experiments, the authors have LLM-based shopping-assistant agents recommend products while manipulating, via the system prompt, whether the agent's stated principal is the consumer or the platform that pays for sponsored listings. Naming the platform as the agent's principal significantly weakens the penalty the agent applies to sponsored listings and dulls the skepticism disclosure normally triggers in its reasoning, a pattern that replicates across multiple LLMs and reasoning depths; sharper disclosure wording ("Sponsored" versus "Promoted") reduces but does not eliminate the gap when the platform is named. The findings suggest that consumer-protection rules built for human-facing disclosures do not automatically transfer to AI-mediated commerce, since the disclosure now reaches an agent whose loyalty is invisible to the consumer it ostensibly serves.
