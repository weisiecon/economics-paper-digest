# AI Economics Papers — 2026-11-28

## 1. Organizing Intelligence [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Alex Imas, Andrew Koh, Shane Legg
**Date:** first posted 2026-10-31
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05417
**Summary:** The authors build a theoretical model in which human and AI agents, each with multidimensional capabilities, sort endogenously into competing firms whose productivity depends on both the competence of their agents and how well the firm coordinates them as it scales. They show that while human-only organizations can still outperform more capable AI agents when human coordination is strong, improvements in AI's own coordination ability can break this "Coasean ceiling" on firm size and tip the market into a phase transition toward a few large, AI-heavy firms. The model also decomposes resulting wage changes into a direct technology effect and an offsetting reorganization effect from firms' shifting size and worker composition, showing the latter can reverse the former.

## 2. Artificial Intelligence and the U.S. Economy: Current Evidence [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Nick Carollo, Leland D. Crane, Daniel Garcia Molina, Joseph Nichols
**Date:** first posted 2026-11-12
**Source:** https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/artificial-intelligence-and-the-us-economy-current-evidence.htm
**Summary:** This Federal Reserve Board staff paper assembles current evidence on how AI-related investment and AI use are affecting the U.S. macroeconomy, covering the data-center investment boom, the pace of consumer and firm adoption, and the knock-on effects on prices in sectors like memory chips. The authors find AI model-producing firms have grown rapidly enough to account for a noticeable share of software-industry gross output, even though most firms still use AI lightly or not at all. They conclude that measured effects on the labor market and on aggregate productivity growth remain small and mixed so far, and that the recent productivity pickup likely reflects factors that predate generative AI's diffusion rather than AI itself.

## Excluded, borderline

- **AI as Shared Business Infrastructure: Firm Growth and the Distribution of Gains** (NBER Working Paper 36003, Barwick, Fang, Liu, Xu & Yuan) — a strong firm-level difference-in-differences study (a Chinese e-commerce platform's AI tool raised small-merchant sales 51.6%, with markets expanding rather than merely reallocating), but NBER's listing gives only "November 2026" with no exact day, consistent with this digest's past exclusion of same-vintage NBER papers on date grounds.
- **Frontier Technology in Frontier Markets? Financing Global AI Entrepreneurship** (CEPR Discussion Paper 22189, Hacheme, Iyoha, Ndiaye & Souleymane) — a large cross-country startup-financing dataset showing AI funding premiums are concentrated in North America and the Middle East and largely absent in Africa and Latin America, but CEPR/IDEAS/EconPapers all give only "2026-11" with no day.
- **Creative destruction: Human-AI competition in an experimental market** (arXiv:2610.18921, Conlon & Schwardmann) — a well-designed three-study experiment (N=1,025) finding AI-designed games outsell human-designed ones, but it was first posted 2026-10-12, outside the 30-day window.
- **Joules-to-GDP: How Much Economic Value Do AI Systems Produce per Unit of Energy?** (arXiv:2610.30858, Zeinali, Saad-Falcon, Narayan, Abbasi, Hartley, Hennessy, Re & Mirhoseini) — a systems/ML benchmarking study across 220 GDPval tasks framed in economic-value terms, but it is a model- and hardware-selection benchmark rather than a market, firm, or aggregate-outcome finding.
- **Uneven Returns from Digitalization and AI: Economic Mechanisms and Policy Implications** (IDB Working Paper, Vlaicu) — a conceptual policy framework on why digital/AI returns cluster unevenly across Latin America and the Caribbean, excluded for lacking both an exact publication day and an empirical or causal research design.
- **What Should We Tax When We Tax AI?** (Brusco & Capozza) — referenced in RePEc's NEP-AIN report of 2026-11-16 but could not be located or verified within this session's search budget.
- Several labor-market-focused items from the same NEP-AIN reports (AI and the Labor Market: Productivity Driver or Job Killer?; Exposure Is Not Adoption; Beyond Exposure: AI and Labor Market Reorganization in Türkiye; The Impact of AI Diffusion on the Labor Market; AI and Skills; Dull, Dirty, Dangerous?) are left to the ai-labor digest rather than duplicated here.
